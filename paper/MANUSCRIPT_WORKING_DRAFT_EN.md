# Distinguishing conformational memory from actin memory in protrusion dynamics

## Abstract

A protrusion can retain a history of stimulation through protein conformational dynamics or through the actin network. Assigning that memory to either process requires observations that distinguish the two. We construct two mechanochemical models with exchanged molecular and actin relaxation times and distinct latent histories of molecular accessibility. A transformation of the molecular state and input couplings gives identical predicted mixed FRET, actin and deformation trajectories for arbitrary inputs. In a separate synthetic test, reversing the pulse order reduced the accuracy of a fixed classifier from 400/400 to 200/400. An additional gate-sensitive measurement with independently bounded actin sensitivity separates the exact pair when their predicted response ranges are disjoint. For illustrative reporter and noise parameters, the probability of a correct assignment was 99.94% at 15-s sampling and 78.52% at 60-s sampling, at the least informative pulse order and integer sampling phase tested. The models are motivated by N-WASP and microglial protrusions. Their discrimination remains a prediction for a calibrated experiment in which molecular accessibility, actin and mechanics are recorded together.

## Introduction

When a protrusion advances after a stimulus has subsided, its motion gives a readout of persistence but leaves the location of that persistence open. In a microglial process, a regulator could remain accessible after its upstream drive falls. Alternatively, the regulator could reset rapidly while the F-actin network that it helped assemble turns over slowly. Either history can sustain an edge response. Here we use *memory* in this kinetic sense, as the slower relaxation following a change in input. The question is whether the same spatially registered measurements can assign this relaxation to the regulator or the network.

N-WASP links this question to a molecular mechanism. Relief of autoinhibition changes the accessibility of its actin-nucleating machinery, while recruitment changes the amount of N-WASP in the membrane-associated reaction volume. Experiments on Nck-mediated activation, membrane curvature and prewetting illustrate distinct ways in which accessibility and local concentration can change actin assembly [1–6]. Conformational disorder can broaden the molecular ensemble sampled by a regulatory region [5,25]. It does not specify the residence time in an accessible state or the relaxation time of a protrusion. We therefore describe a coarse accessibility coordinate with its own kinetics and distinguish it from the locally available protein and from the actin network to which it couples. In this description a slow molecular response and slow actin remodeling are explicit rivals.

Each experimental channel projects a different part of this sequence. A fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) reporter depends on its fluorophore geometry and the sensor's response to the molecular environment. An actin reporter samples a labeled pool that can reflect binding and turnover as well as assembly. Tip displacement integrates network activity with mechanical resistance and coupling to the environment. A traction measurement instead estimates the stress transmitted to a substrate through a specified mechanical inverse problem [7,14]. These observables constrain different steps and cannot be substituted for each other without a model of how they are generated.

An unpublished proteomic dataset from CD11b-enriched mouse brain provides background for considering N-WASP in this setting [26]. The models below use specified kinetic parameters. They make a separate prediction about measurements of N-WASP accessibility, local abundance, F-actin and mechanics taken together at a protrusion. To locate the source of persistence, we first ask whether two models with different assignments of the slow relaxation can produce the same joint observations. We then challenge a classifier trained on a restricted synthetic family by changing the order of stimulation. This motivates an independently calibrated reporter and a prospective paired test in microglia. Two distinct feedback models then establish the difference between local instability and autonomous chaotic behavior.

## Methods

### Exact equivalence of the two linear models

We represent conformational accessibility, actin assembly and deformation by the dimensionless deviations \(q,a,x\) from a common resting state. Each state relaxes toward a moving target. In model A, a molecular input \(u\) moves the target for accessibility, an assembly input \(v\) enters the actin balance, and deformation follows the assembly state. With time in assigned seconds and zero initial deviations,

\[
8\dot q=u-q,\qquad 25\dot a=q+v-a,\qquad 20\dot x=a-x.
\]

The optical precursor is \(f=rq+(1-r)a\), where \(r=8/25=0.32\). This choice makes the ambiguity exact. Set \(q'=f\). Multiplying its derivative by 25 and substituting the first two balances gives \(25\dot q'=u-q+(1-r)(q+v-a)=u+(1-r)v-q'\). The identity \(q'-a=r(q-a)\) then yields a second realization,

\[
25\dot q'=u+(1-r)v-q',\qquad 8\dot a=q'+rv-a,\qquad 20\dot x=a-x.
\]

Model B reports \(f'=q'\). The transformation exchanges the molecular and actin relaxation times while preserving both the assembly state that drives deformation and the optical precursor. It is invertible because \(r>0\). With corresponding initial states the two realizations therefore agree on \((f,a,x)\) for every common input history. They differ in where \(v\) enters, a distinction that matters if its molecular selectivity is known independently. The 20-s deformation law is an effective relaxation of shape driven by assembly. Its coefficient represents neither force nor a measured material modulus.

### Joint observation and synthetic perturbation challenge

We asked how well joint observation resolves an ordering of relaxation times when the reporter and input couplings are more tightly restricted. Within each independent parameter draw, the two kinetic families exchanged one fast time (uniform on 6–12 s) and one slow time (30–50 s) between accessibility and assembly. They shared a deformation time (12–30 s) and reporter times (2–6 s each). All five states started at 0.5. For the accessibility reporter state \(s\) and actin reporter state \(l\), the balances were

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tau_q\dot q&=(1+e^{-(u+\beta v)})^{-1}-q, &
\tau_a\dot a&=q+\alpha u+0.20v-a,\\
\tau_x\dot x&=a+\chi v-x, &
\tau_s\dot s&=q-s,\qquad \tau_l\dot l=a-l .
\end{aligned}
\]

The unit inputs occupied [20,80) and [140,200) s, respectively. Four conditions comprised sham, either pulse alone, and both pulses in sequence. Uniform cross-effects allowed \(u\) to enter the assembly balance (\(|\alpha|\le0.03\)) and \(v\) to enter the accessibility and deformation balances (\(|\beta|\le0.20, |\chi|\le0.03\)). The model assigns no molecular target to these interventions.

To distinguish biochemical mixing from optical contamination, we generated photon counts after reporter relaxation. Write \(E=0.45-0.20\tanh[2(s-0.5)]\) for the effective transfer efficiency and \(G_i(t)=g_i N\exp(-b_i t)\) for the gain and bleaching factor of channel \(i\). Expected donor, acceptor and actin counts were

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_D &=40+G_D(1-E),\\
\mu_A &=40+G_A[E+c(1-E)+kl],\\
\mu_L &=40+G_L(l+jE).
\end{aligned}
\]

Each parent used a common photon scale \(N\) uniformly drawn from 1,500 to 3,000. For each channel, gain \(g_i\) was uniform on [0.85, 1.15] and bleaching rate \(b_i\) was uniform on [0, 0.00025] per assigned second. Here \(c\le0.04\) represents donor leakage and \(k,j\le0.03\) represent cross-channel contributions, all nonnegative. Each count comprised a Poisson draw plus independent Gaussian read noise (SD 2 counts). We subtracted the known background of 40 counts before forming the acceptor/donor ratio and normalizing actin intensity to its initial value. The deformation channel carried independent additive Gaussian noise (SD 0.005 in normalized shape units). Subtraction of the initial observation couples the subsequent features within a trajectory. The joint vector contained 16 post-baseline optical values per reporter and five deformation values at 60-s intervals.

Development and reserved test sets contained 300 and 200 independent parameter parents, respectively. The two kinetic families and four input conditions remained grouped within a parent; development and test used separate random streams. The sham and bootstrap calculations also used independent streams. For each non-sham condition and channel set, we standardized features using development means and standard deviations and assigned each trace to the nearest family centroid in squared Mahalanobis distance, adding 0.1 times the identity to the pooled within-family covariance. The fixed primary comparison was joint versus deformation-only accuracy under both inputs, with paired parent bootstrap intervals. A distinct no-input sham set tested a threshold learned from training sham FRET trajectories; an equal-time input control had no ground-truth kinetic ordering. The independently specified linear comparison was fixed before seeing challenge outcomes and checked with exact matrix exponentials. RK4 used a 0.25-s step with input boundaries aligned to steps; a nominal step-halving comparison changed states by at most 2.27 × 10⁻⁸. State bounds and nonnegative photon means were checked, and no denominator floors were encountered. These are local numerical and synthetic checks, not calibration of the microscope, an exhaustive model comparison or a cellular validation.

### Pulse-order stress and calibrated readout

We used the joint classifier fitted to the same 300 development parameter sets, keeping the features, covariance regularization and sampling times fixed. An independent sample of 200 parameter sets supplied both family assignments and four conditions per set. The previously used order u then v was compared with the independent order v then u, with u and v in [20,80) and [140,200) or exchanged. A wider combined stress simultaneously set α in [−0.12,0.12], β in [−0.6,0.6], χ in [−0.12,0.12], channel bleed in [0,0.20] and other optical cross-channel terms in [0,0.15], and drove the sensor filter with 0.5q+0.5a. The same parents were retained across conditions; noise draws were condition-specific. We calculated equal-weight family accuracy and parent-paired bootstrap intervals from 5,000 resamples. This design tests the frozen, time-indexed rule, rather than information-theoretic identifiability under all estimators. States and intensities were checked for finiteness and nonnegativity; no ratio denominator was floored.

For the independent readout design, we propagated the linear twin using a matrix exponential at 1-s intervals from 0 to 300 s with pulse amplitudes of 0.3 for u and 0.2 for v, both pulse orders and zero initial deviations. We evaluated sensor response times of 0, 10 and 30 assigned seconds (zero denotes instantaneous response), cadences of 5, 15 and 60 s with all integer phases, and bounds \(b=0.20,0.50,0.68\). Let \(H\) apply the unit-gain first-order reporter filter and then sample at the specified times. For a reporter with actin sensitivity \(m\), \(0\le m\le b\), subtraction of \(Ha\) leaves \(y=H[(1-m)(q-a)]+\epsilon\). Write \(g=H(q_A-a)\). Since \(q_B-a=r(q_A-a)\), the two possible mean traces are \(c_Ag\) and \(c_Bg\), with \(c_A\in[1-b,1]\) and \(c_B\in[r(1-b),r]\). Each model may take any sensitivity consistent with the independent calibration. Under independent Gaussian noise of SD \(\sigma=0.03\) per sample, the scalar projection \(g^\top y/(\sigma\lVert g\rVert)\) has unit variance. For the bounds tested, the nearest means are separated by

\[
d=(1-b-r)\lVert g\rVert/\sigma.
\]

We used the smallest template norm over both pulse orders and all integer phases at each cadence. After centering the scalar at the nearest model-B mean, the two nearest means are 0 and \(d\). With \(t=\max(d/2,1.95996)\), we declared A above \(t\), B below \(d-t\), and abstained between them. At either nearest mean, correct and wrong declaration probabilities are \(\Phi(d-t)\) and \(\Phi(-t)\), respectively, where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function. We checked these probabilities with 10,000 independent Gaussian realizations per family and setting (seed 202611180301). Adaptive integration split at pulse boundaries independently agreed with the matrix-exponential filtered contrast to \(1.61\times10^{-11}\) in the original order at \(\tau=10\) s. The sensitivity estimates apply to the specified synthetic signal, noise and acquisition times. An experimental power calculation would require measured reporter kinetics and noise.

### Separating instrumental calibration from state sensitivity

For the separate synthetic reference calibration, we represented the two recorded intensities by \(\mathbf y=M\mathbf w+\mathbf b\), where \(\mathbf w\) contains the source intensities, \(M\) is the optical transfer matrix and \(\mathbf b\) is the known background. We fitted each column of \(M\) on 60 single-component standards at known source intensities drawn uniformly between 500 and 3,000 counts, using least squares with a fixed background of 40 counts and Poisson photon noise. For each of 100 reserved mixed standards we compared the ratio from the background-subtracted channels with the ratio after linear unmixing,

\[
\widehat{\mathbf w}=\widehat M^{-1}(\mathbf y-\mathbf b),\qquad
R=w_2/w_1.
\]

The endpoint was the median of \(\lvert\widehat R/R-1\rvert\). The calibration estimates photon-channel mixing given specified sources. The coefficient \(m\) instead specifies how the molecular reporter responds to a cellular actin state within the observation model.

### Molecular availability and feedback dynamics

The organization model distinguishes local N-WASP amount \(p\) in a reaction volume \(V\) from the accessible fraction \(q\). The normalized active pool \(z=(p/V)q\) drives a saturating response \(H(z)=z^2/(1+z^2)\) in the spatial organization calculations. The stationary availability example uses the separate prescribed downstream balance detailed in the Supporting Information. We use the invariance of the product \(z\) to compare distinct ways of setting the activity available to actin. At fixed volume, an amount increase can compensate for reduced accessibility.

To examine how the same coupling might matter dynamically, we contrasted the feed-forward cascade with an equal-time feedback model. All times in this calculation are in assigned model units. With \(\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}\), ensemble bias \(z_0\), and mechanical inhibition \(g\), its equations are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot q&=\sigma(z_0-gx)-q,\\
\dot a&=q-a,\qquad \dot x=a-x.
\end{aligned}
\]

At equilibrium \(q_*=a_*=x_*\). The slope of the sigmoid at the equilibrium gives a feedback gain \(K=gq_*(1-q_*)\). The linearized loop has characteristic equation \((\lambda+1)^3+K=0\). For \(K\ge0\) its leading real part is \(-1+K^{1/3}/2\). This eigenvalue tests local stability. It does not specify the long-time dynamics beyond the instability.

A separate, deliberately Lorenz-derived benchmark asks what follows when the autonomous feedback is specifically constructed to support aperiodic dynamics. The internal coordinates \(U,V,W\) are signed deviations assigned to conformation, actin regulation and feedback. These labels specify the interpretation of the construction. In particular \(V\) in this benchmark is a signed dynamic coordinate, distinct from the fixed reaction volume above. The model and displacement filter are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot U&=10(V-U)+\eta\tanh(U/10),\\
\dot V&=U(r-W)-V,\\
\dot W&=UV-\tfrac83 W,\\
\dot x&=[\sigma((W-25)/30)-x]/2 .
\end{aligned}
\]

We compared \(\eta=0.5\) at \(r=10\) and \(r=28\). Bounded proxies \(q=\sigma(U/20)\) and \(m_{\mathrm{fb}}=\sigma((W-25)/30)\) display the state projection. We integrated the full-state variational equation with periodic QR orthonormalization over 1,000 assigned time units after a transient of 200 units, estimating each exponent from the cumulative logarithm of the corresponding diagonal stretch divided by the accumulation time. The Supporting Information records the alternative initial conditions, time-step check, independent adaptive integrator and separate analytical limit for the canonical Lorenz system. These calculations test dynamical feasibility in this circuit.

## Results

### Distinct conformational histories produce the same observations

We placed the slower relaxation in either the molecular gate or the actin network, then compared their joint predictions under the same pair of inputs (Figure 1, panel a). In the exact linear models, \(q\) and \(a\) denote deviations from baseline molecular accessibility and effective actin assembly. In model A these states relax on timescales of 8 and 25 assigned seconds, respectively. The fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) signal before optical mixing is represented by \(f=0.32q+0.68a\). Model B uses the swapped times and the transformed gate coordinate \(q'=0.32q+0.68a\), with correspondingly changed input coupling. The two inputs are nominally directed at molecular accessibility (u) and actin assembly (v). For common inputs, both generate identical pre-optical FRET, actin and deformation trajectories, although q and q′ differ. Matrix-exponential calculations gave maximum discrepancies of 2.23 × 10⁻¹⁶, 1.67 × 10⁻¹⁶ and 8.33 × 10⁻¹⁷ for those three readouts. The maximum latent-gate difference was 0.126 in normalized state units (Figure 1b). The mixed FRET, actin and deformation trajectories overlay for that same input (Figure 1c). An independent exact-rational substitution verified the transformed differential equations for arbitrary u and v; any common downstream optical map and noise distribution preserves their observational equivalence. The 68% pre-optical state mixing and associated input coupling lie outside the narrow nuisance ranges used in the classification test below; they are an adversarial mathematical possibility, not an estimate of reporter bleed-through or evidence of a real microglial mechanism.

We also tested classification under a restricted observation model with separately specified inputs. Two paired families exchanged the gate and actin relaxation times, while sharing the remaining parameters within each synthetic parent. A gate-directed input u, an actin-directed input v, both inputs and sham were modeled as separate conditions; each was allowed to affect more than its nominal target. The observation model generated donor and acceptor photon counts, a separate actin-reporter count and dimensionless deformation. Sensor delays, different bleaching rates, limited optical mixing, photon noise and read noise were assumed. The inputs and time units were assigned rather than measured, and the optical model does not capture every property of a real reporter.

With u alone, classification on reserved simulated cases from the two families was 208/400 (52.0%) using deformation, 398/400 (99.5%) using FRET, 191/400 (47.75%) using the actin reporter and 399/400 (99.75%) using all three channels (Figure 1d). FRET recovered 199/200 cases in each family; the 95% Wilson interval for either count is 97.2–99.9%. With v alone, the corresponding accuracies were 389/400 (97.25%), 203/400 (50.75%), 400/400 and 400/400. Under both inputs, deformation alone and the joint readout yielded 398/400 (99.5%) and 400/400 (100%), respectively; the parent-bootstrap interval for the 0.5-percentage-point gain was 0–1.25 points. The 400 cases in each condition comprise two model outcomes for each of 200 independently simulated parameter sets, not 400 biological replicates. In this narrow generator, the actin-directed input can reveal a downstream timescale through deformation; high classification accuracy does not imply that deformation identified a protein conformation.

A separate FRET-change detector was calibrated on no-input training cases and applied without adjustment to new sham cases. It labeled 15/200 shams positive (7.5%; 95% Wilson interval 4.6–12.0%), and detected 199/200 and 159/200 cases in the two families under both inputs. These are properties of the declared synthetic detector, not biological false-positive or sensitivity estimates; the interval is conditional on the learned threshold. In a separate equal-time control, where no fast-versus-slow ordering existed, the forced joint classifier assigned the fast label to 129/200 and 135/200 cases in the two arbitrarily labeled copies. It had no abstention mechanism, so these labels cannot be interpreted as a mechanistic false-positive rate.

### A reversed pulse order changes the classification outcome

The exact observation twin suggested that the favorable in-family classifier depended on restrictions on sensor mixing and intervention cross-effects. We therefore reconstructed its original training set and froze the learned classifiers before generating 200 new, independent parameter sets simulated under both models. With the original order of gate-directed input followed by actin-directed input, the joint rule classified all 400 model assignments correctly. Reversing the order without refitting reduced this to 200/400 (50%, Figure 1e): all 400 assignments were called fast, including all 200 slow-family cases. The parent-paired change was −50 percentage points, with a bootstrap interval of −50 to −50 points for this finite synthetic cohort. This zero-width interval reflects identical parent-level outcomes, not zero uncertainty about new pulse families or biological performance. The deformation-only classifier also assigned all reversed-order cases to the fast class. The experiment identifies failure of this fixed time-indexed decision rule under the specified distribution shift; it does not establish that the inverted inputs contain no information or that a classifier trained across orders could not resolve them.

In a separate combined stress condition with the original pulse order, wider assumed perturbation cross-effects and optical mixing, plus a FRET-state readout driven equally by q and actin, the joint rule remained correct in 400/400 assignments; the deformation-only rule fell to 319/400 (79.75%). Under the reversed order and the same combined stress, both classifiers again assigned all cases to the fast class. The stress changes several quantities together, so their individual effects are not identifiable from this comparison. All results concern the specified synthetic families; a nominally targeted input is not established as selective in cells.

### Calibration and cadence bound separation of the gate–actin twin

Because the exact pair shares its original readouts under either pulse order, we asked whether an additional molecular observation could exclude the rival mean within independently determined limits on its sensitivity (Figure 2, panel a). We therefore extended the observation model with an independently calibrated readout \(z=h_\tau*[(1-m)q+ma]+\epsilon\), where \(h_\tau\) is a unit-gain first-order sensor response and \(m\) is the fraction of the unit response attributable to actin. We assume an independent calibration bounds \(0\le m\le b\) and allow each rival to use a different value within that bound. After subtraction of the common filtered actin template, the expected trace is a multiple of \(h_\tau*(q_A-a)\). The coefficient ranges are \([1-b,1]\) for model A and \([0.32(1-b),0.32]\) for model B. They meet at \(b=0.68\) and overlap above it (Figure 2b). Below 0.68, their separation can be resolved only if the sampled template has sufficient signal relative to noise. The 0.68 boundary is specific to the constructed twin and the assumed normalization. Neither an empirical cross-sensitivity nor an optical bleed-through coefficient enters this calculation.

For a single synthetic trace, with sensor time constant 10 assigned seconds, independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.03 in normalized readout units and \(b=0.20\), the rule identified the correct rival with probability 99.94% at 15 s and 78.52% at 60 s (Figure 2c). These estimates use the closest allowed model means and the least informative pair of pulse order and sampled integer phase at each cadence. Increasing the cross-sensitivity bound to 0.50 reduced the 15-s result to 67.57%. A longer sensor response reduced correct declarations at 60-s sampling under the same noise assumptions (Figure 2d). The rule can abstain, and the decomposition at 15 s shows how abstentions account for most of the loss in correct declarations as the cross-sensitivity bound rises (Figure 2e). At the touching means (\(b=0.68\)), 5.30% of 10,000 independently simulated noise draws produced either declaration, against the analytical 5% rate. This null concerns the touching model means only. The calculation assumes known latent templates, known response times and independent noise. It motivates measuring actin cross-sensitivity and reporter kinetics in the pilot before choosing cadence. The experimental resolution of either model remains undetermined.

We evaluated optical mixing separately because a corrected photon ratio still need not read the gate specifically. In 100 withheld synthetic mixtures, the median absolute relative ratio error fell from 21.1% to 3.95% after applying the independently estimated instrument matrix (Figure 2f). The true off-diagonal terms were 0.15 and 0.25, and their estimates were 0.151 and 0.250. The calculation establishes what single-source references can recover for the declared linear instrument. Establishing \(m\), the response kinetics and the intervention cross-effects requires information about the biological states.

### From molecular accessibility to mechanically coupled dynamics

The same molecular-to-mechanical path that transmits an input may also return a load-dependent signal (Figure 3, panel a). Before asking whether such a loop changes the regime of motion, there is a more local ambiguity. At unit reaction volume the two stationary combinations \((p,q)=(0.5,0.5)\) and \((1,0.25)\) both produce \(z=0.25\) and the same model actin and load values of 0.3723 (Figure 3b). Changes in recruitment and accessibility can therefore compensate at steady state. Local abundance is a separate observable in a test of conformational memory.

In the feed-forward cascade, differences between trajectories driven by the same prescribed inputs decay asymptotically. With equal relaxation times and a closed negative feedback loop, changing the equilibrium accessibility at fixed \(g=40\) from 0.10 to 0.50 moves \(K\) from 3.6 to 10. The leading real part changes from −0.234 to +0.0772 per assigned time unit (Figure 3c). The unstable roots form a complex pair. The result establishes an oscillatory linear instability of the chosen equilibrium, without determining whether the nonlinear response settles into oscillation or another regime.

The distinct Lorenz-derived circuit supplies a boundary case for autonomous irregular dynamics (Figure 3d). At \(\eta=0.5\) and \(r=28\), the largest full-state finite-time Lyapunov estimate was 0.9036 per assigned time unit. The independent adaptive integrator gave 0.9076. At \(r=10\) the trajectory approached a fixed point with estimate −0.5097. The gate–feedback projection illustrates the corresponding recurrence at \(r=28\). The positive estimate belongs to the four-dimensional deterministic state and to this constructed feedback law. It gives neither a Lyapunov estimate from a single cellular trajectory nor a calibration of conformational entropy.

## Discussion

### Two kinds of ambiguity in the same experiment

There are two distinct reasons why a fitted curve can give the wrong location of memory. The exact construction permits a change in latent coordinates together with a change in input couplings and reporter sensitivity. It preserves all three outputs algebraically, independent of noise or pulse schedule. In the restricted family, reducing the cross-effects and optical nuisance creates class information that a time-indexed estimator can exploit. Its failure after pulse reversal exposes reliance on the original schedule. Altering the schedule therefore probes transfer of the fitted rule, whereas establishing independent bounds on measurement and perturbation couplings can restrict the equivalence itself. The construction concerns the chosen network and its reparameterization. An intervention verified to couple selectively to a physical state would provide a constraint outside that equivalence.

### What a microglial experiment would have to resolve

The three biological quantities enter the interpretation in sequence. A rise in local N-WASP changes the pool available for activation; a change in conformational occupancy changes the accessible fraction of that pool; and the actin network can retain a response after either signal returns toward baseline. Under the slow-gate hypothesis, a selective gate perturbation would leave a delayed relaxation in a calibrated accessibility trace after accounting for local protein amount and actin-dependent sensor response. Under the slow-actin hypothesis, accessibility would recover earlier, while the actin signal and the downstream mechanical response would persist. Either interpretation is conditional on measuring the specificity and kinetics of the perturbation and reporter. If the uncertainty ranges of the rival joint predictions still overlap after calibration, the outcome is unresolved at that assay resolution.

Published actin studies sampled at intervals of 6 and 15 s in different preparations [12,13]. A primary rat microglial traction study recorded substrate beads at 30-s intervals [14]. An experiment that combines multiple reporters and mechanics must establish its own timing, including the delay between channels.

The proposed pilot pairs a proxy for local N-WASP amount, a conformation-sensitive signal, an F-actin reporter and a mechanical measurement within the same protrusion. It establishes optical mixing and bleaching with references [19], then evaluates reporter response, biological cross-effects and noise using calibration preparations. Traction, where available, is estimated from substrate displacement with an independently characterized substrate and relaxed reference. Projected motion can be recorded as a separate geometric endpoint. Preparation is the independent unit for a later test, with ROIs and cells nested within preparation. A pulse schedule and decision rule fixed before acquisition of those reserved preparations would distinguish calibration from confirmation. The theory supplies the separation criterion. The pilot supplies the inputs to judge whether the criterion can resolve the two models in cells.

### From conformational disorder to irregular motion

Assigning a slow response is a prerequisite for a stronger claim that molecular disorder reorganizes protrusion dynamics. A conformational ensemble can alter a regulator's accessibility or response time. Irregular movement can also arise through fluctuating input, actin network dynamics or feedback through mechanical resistance [21,22]. In the feed-forward model, differences between trajectories driven by the same prescribed input decay. An aperiodic output under external drive therefore need not imply autonomous instability. The supplementary feedback model shows how a shift in ensemble bias can cross a linear stability boundary, and the deliberately Lorenz-derived system records mathematical feasibility of autonomous chaos in a constructed circuit. Neither gives a measured dependence of cellular dynamics on conformational entropy. A biological test of that stronger link would first need the state assignment and mechanical pairing developed here, and would then have to discriminate endogenous feedback from driven or stochastic alternatives.

## Conclusion

Two mechanochemical models can share FRET, actin and deformation trajectories while assigning slow relaxation to different cellular processes. For the constructed pair, constraining reporter sensitivity narrows the allowed response sets and defines when the sampled contrast can distinguish them. This condition turns the interpretation of protrusion memory into a prospective question for paired, independently calibrated microglial recordings.

## Supplementary Results

### Actin assembly and deformation under constant, periodic and stochastic inputs

The prior Lorenz-based feedback construction established a mathematical possibility but did not derive its signed quadratic interactions from a cellular mechanism. We therefore constructed a separate model comprising a two-state accessibility fraction q, an effective actin-assembly fraction a, an overdamped deformation x and a tension-sensor proxy s. With F=kx and B=b+u−αF, the gate rates are \(k_\pm=k_0e^{\pm B/2}\) and \(\dot q=k_+(1-q)-k_-q\). The assembly and mechanical equations are \(\dot a=k_pq(1-a)(1+\rho a)-k_da\) and \(\zeta\dot x=F_pa-kx\); the separate observation obeys \(\tau_s\dot s=E_0-gF-s\). At fixed F and u, the gate rate ratio is e^B and its stationary accessible fraction is sigmoid(B). The rate-barrier split, sign of force feedback, autocatalytic assembly and linear sensor response are hypotheses, not measurements. The active force has no resolved chemical fuel or whole-system energy balance; s is a tension-sensor proxy, not the molecular gate q.

For one prespecified dimensionless parameter set, the autonomous model approached the unique equilibrium (q,a,x)=(0.4885,0.6149,0.6149), whose Jacobian had largest real part −0.6382 per assigned time unit. A weighted-norm bound yields a stronger result for this parameter set: all four states contract at a rate of at least 0.05 under the same prescribed input. This bounded regime therefore cannot produce autonomous chaos. Keeping the parameters fixed, a known periodic input and a stochastic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck input yielded post-transient standard deviations of load 0.0323 and 0.0226, respectively, whereas the autonomous trajectory became effectively constant (Supplementary Figure 1). The stochastic input represents an external fluctuating drive, not necessarily intrinsic molecular noise. Neither input was fitted to the experimental data, and the three trajectories do not constitute a power analysis or a mechanistic classifier.

We next examined a public biological observation with a closer, but incomplete, connection to the proposed bridge. Their source workbook provides representative paired traces. They do not equate molecular tension with mean substrate traction, measure the conformational state q of N-WASP, or provide an actin-assembly time series in the same microglial protrusion. The model's inverse F–s observation was imposed by construction and cannot be validated by the sign alone. Both a deterministic periodic drive and a stochastic drive gave inverse synthetic F–s associations in this model, so that relationship alone does not identify an autonomous oscillator or protein gate.

### N-WASP abundance and localization leave different conformations compatible with the same output

The [Human Protein Atlas v21 WASL entry](https://v21.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000106299-WASL/single+cell+type/brain) reports 34.7 nTPM in a brain microglial cluster containing 206 cells. This normalized cluster aggregate establishes expression context; it does not measure protein copy number, report independent donor variability, or resolve the accessible fraction. In a separate larval zebrafish study, [Mazzolini and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1002/glia.24154) measured a 1.46-fold wasla RNA increase by qPCR in sorted mCherry-positive microglia/macrophages after mosaic wasla manipulation, and observed altered microglial motility in a pre-neoplastic setting. The RNA and motility measurements were not a paired conformational–actin–force recording; neither value is a measurement of human N-WASP protein or accessibility.

We also generated a hypothetical protein readout using two assumed RNA distributions nominally centered on 34.7 nTPM, a broad relative-protein scale, uncertain RNA–protein elasticity, and a specified multiplicative observation error. The central 95% range for the simulated relative protein readout was 0.054–17.67 under an assumed RNA geometric standard deviation of 2, and 0.020–45.56 under an assumed value of 10. In the implemented equation the number 34.7 cancels exactly when RNA is divided by its reference: the atlas therefore provides expression context but no quantitative constraint on the simulated protein distribution. These intervals reflect the chosen distributions and arbitrary reference; they are not measured N-WASP concentrations, single-cell variability estimates or posteriors.

We introduced a separate extension in which total protein P, tip partition p and accessible fraction q determine the effective local pool \(m=Pp/(VK)\) and active proxy \(z=mq\). The actin production term is now \(k_p[z/(1+z)](1-a)(1+\rho a)\); gate kinetics, mechanics and the separate tension-sensor proxy are retained. The response law and parameters are assumed, and the original model's contraction result does not automatically apply to this extension. The published RNA ratio 1.46 served as a hypothetical input, not as a matched human protein measurement. We assumed \(P_1/P_0=1.46^\beta\) and \(m_1/m_0=1.46^\beta\xi\), where β is an unspecified RNA–protein transfer and ξ a relative localization/volume factor. With baseline m=1 and β=1, ξ=1 yielded an equilibrium load change of +0.0628, whereas ξ=0.5 yielded −0.0588. The same hypothetical total-protein change can therefore support opposite load directions in this model. The change in the gate fraction is also conditional on the assumed negative load feedback, not an observed N-WASP closure.

Across 20,000 draws for each broad assumed prior, with β uniformly distributed between zero and one and baseline m log-uniform between 0.1 and 10, widening the log-uniform ξ range from 0.1–10 to 0.01–100 changed the central 95% prior-predictive interval for the load difference from [−0.320, 0.368] to [−0.566, 0.531]. For these parameters the equilibrium load is strictly increasing in m, so its difference is positive exactly when \(\xi>1.46^{-\beta}\). The 54.5% and 51.9% positive draws follow the geometry of the specified priors; they are not posterior probabilities or experimental confidence intervals. The 1.46 RNA ratio was fixed without propagating its experimental uncertainty. In a subsequent specified dynamic exercise with m fixed at 0.73, 1 or 1.46, the load contrasts relative to baseline were −0.0587/+0.0629 under periodic input and −0.0578/+0.0613 for one common stochastic-input realization. Inputs and output time units are dimensionless; these examples are not a biological validation or a prospective independent test.

The observational ambiguity can be exact even without measurement noise. For P=V=K=1, the two stationary states (p,q)=(0.5,0.5) and (1,0.25), with different gate biases, both have z=0.25, a=0.3723 and F=0.3723, and therefore the same assumed mechanical sensor output. This equivalence concerns stationary observations in this model; appropriately designed temporal perturbations may distinguish them. It illustrates why a global omics measurement, even perfectly measured, cannot on its own calibrate local conformation or assign the origin of persistence in a protrusion.

### A constructed feedback benchmark admits autonomous chaos

The existing feed-forward gate–actin–mechanics cascade forgets initial differences under the same prescribed input, as shown below. To test whether a reciprocal alternative can exhibit autonomous chaos, we introduced a distinct feedback topology rather than retuning that cascade. The dimensionless state variables U, V and W represent an effective signed conformational coordinate, an actin-regulatory deviation and a mechanical feedback coordinate; an additional filtered variable x represents normalized displacement. With constant coefficients and no external time-varying drive or stochastic input, we used

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot U&=10(V-U)+\eta\tanh(U/10),\\
\dot V&=U(r-W)-V,\\
\dot W&=UV-\tfrac83 W,\\
\dot x&=[\sigma((W-25)/30)-x]/2.
\end{aligned}
\]

The bounded proxies q=σ(U/20), a=σ(V/30) and m=σ((W−25)/30) serve as illustrative observations of the gate, actin and feedback states. The internal signed variables and their sigmoidal mappings are not measured concentrations or forces. When η=0, the three-state core is the established Lorenz system [8]; the term at η=0.5 adds a specified bounded self-feedback. This reverse-engineered benchmark was constructed to permit autonomous chaos and is not a newly derived molecular or mechanical law.

For η=0.5 and r=28, the largest full-state finite-time Lyapunov exponent was 0.9036 per assigned time unit for the reference initial condition, with 0.9068 and 0.9011 for two additional initial conditions (Supplementary Figure 2). The corresponding second exponents were near zero, and the other two were negative. In the reference case, the four exponents were approximately (0.9036, −0.0004, −0.5083, −14.5280); their sum differed from the time-averaged Jacobian trace by about 0.0001. Halving the Runge–Kutta step gave 0.9051, and an independently implemented adaptive DOP853 integration gave 0.9076. At r=10 and η=0.5, the trajectory approached a fixed point with largest exponent −0.5097. These are finite numerical estimates for specified initial states, parameters and a constructed deterministic model, not measurements of cells or a proof that all nearby parameter values are chaotic.

The canonical reference also admits a conditional analytical interpretation. Tucker proved the existence of the classical Lorenz attractor [9]. For any complete trajectory on that compact invariant set, the linearly filtered variable x has a unique bounded history defined by a convergent exponentially weighted integral. Its graph is continuous and invariant, and the joint flow restricted to the graph is topologically conjugate to the Lorenz base. Two x values driven by the same base trajectory converge as \(e^{-t/2}\). This argument uses the published theorem and the specified one-way filter; it is not a new independent proof of the Lorenz attractor, nor a proof for the modified η=0.5 base. A full derivation and its conditions are recorded with the study.

The observation map has an additional exact limitation. The transformation \((U,V,W,x)\mapsto(-U,-V,W,x)\) preserves the declared equations for every r and η. Symmetry-related trajectories therefore have identical W and x histories when started with the same x, while their gate proxies are q and 1−q. Consequently, an ideal measurement of x alone cannot distinguish these two model trajectories. This is an algebraic property of the assumed model, not evidence that a real protein or microglial protrusion obeys this symmetry.

The numerical example supports a bounded conceptual possibility: an autonomous feedback generator can drive an irregular mechanical proxy even if a downstream output attenuates its variation. Its mechanochemical interpretation remains a hypothesis. We represent a proposed ensemble contribution as \(r=r_0+\kappa[\log(\Omega_A/\Omega_I)-\Delta E/(k_BT)]\). At fixed r, shifting the bracket by d and r₀ by −κd leaves every trajectory unchanged; measurements of the outputs alone cannot separate that ensemble bias from the baseline coupling. The gate, actin and mechanical assignments also inherit the Lorenz core’s symmetry and signed bilinear terms, which are not derived from mass-action chemistry. Thus the positive exponent is not evidence that protein conformational entropy causes chaos, and the difference between r=10 and r=28 cannot be attributed uniquely to entropy.

### Molecular redistribution can increase or decrease predicted actin assembly

Consider two equal-area compartments with regulator concentrations m(1−r) and m(1+r), where m is their dimensionless mean and 0≤r≤1 is the contrast. The mean concentration remains m for every r. For the illustrative saturating local response h(c)=c²/(1+c²), the difference between spatially averaged response and uniform response is

\[
\Delta H=\frac{d^2(1+d^2-3m^2)}{(1+m^2)[1+(m-d)^2][1+(m+d)^2]},\qquad d=mr.
\]

The denominator is positive; the numerator permits either sign. Thus concentration heterogeneity can raise the mean response in one regime and lower it in another without changing total regulator. This statement is a direct consequence of the curvature of the chosen response law, not evidence of a particular molecular assembly. In contrast, the non-cooperative saturating law h(c)=c/(1+c) is concave and cannot increase its spatial mean under such a redistribution.

We propagated this effect through the same downstream model of effective actin output and deformation for all three organizational descriptions. For the representative input, recruitment and the demixing analogue produced similar late contrast but distinct onset and relaxation dynamics (Supplementary Figure 3). At m=0.3, heterogeneous organization increased the effective actin output and displacement relative to uniform regulation; at m=1.0, the same organization reduced the late actin output and displacement. All quantities are dimensionless model outputs, and the depicted trajectories are not measured cellular forces or protrusion lengths.

### Membrane recruitment can reproduce the response predicted by demixing

The high in-family classification accuracy with contrast does not imply that the contrast uniquely diagnoses phase separation. For a contrast trajectory r_C(t) produced by the demixing analogue, define a time-dependent recruitment target

\[
s(t)=r_C(t)+\frac{\tau_r\dot r_C(t)}{1-r_C(t)^2},\quad |r_C(t)|<1.
\]

The convex recruitment equation \(\dot r=(1-r^2)[s(t)-r]/\tau_r\), initialized at the same contrast, then admits r_C(t) as a solution. Since the downstream actin and mechanical equations are also identical, they admit the same b(t) and x(t). This is a conditional mathematical construction: it permits an independently driven recruitment target chosen with knowledge of the demixing trajectory. It does not show that such a target was present in a cell or will remain matched after a new perturbation.

In a post hoc numerical illustration at the two representative concentrations, the constructed target ranged from 0.0298 to 0.6525 at its stored half-second points. An independently integrated recruitment trajectory differed from the saved demixing solution by at most 3.43 × 10⁻⁵ across contrast, actin output and deformation at the sampled times. This is a constructed observational equivalence, not an independent prospective test. A first implementation used the wrong sign in the target and did not reproduce the trajectory; that attempt was retained and the algebraic sign corrected before this illustration.

### A spatial perturbation separates restricted passive laws but not active feedback

We extended the organization layer to a one-dimensional periodic field with conserved mean regulator. An imposed zero-mean redistribution was the same at time zero for a Cahn–Hilliard demixing model, passive recruitment relaxation and a phenomenological active recruitment alternative without an imposed passive demixing free energy; the same local actin and mechanical readouts followed all three fields. We fixed a contrast ratio G₆₀ above 1.3 as the criterion for a demixing label before generating the new synthetic cases. At each of the two assumed mean concentrations, the rule labeled 200/200 demixing cases, 0/200 passive-recruitment cases and 200/200 active-recruitment cases as demixing. For 200/200 or 0/200, the corresponding two-sided 95% Wilson interval reaches approximately 98.1–100% or 0–1.9%, respectively. These are repeated synthetic parents under the specified distributions; their intervals do not describe microglial variability. Thus contrast separated the two restricted passive descriptions but could not separate the specified active alternative.

The same fields changed simulated actin output and effective load. At m=0.3, the median 60-s force change was +6.43×10⁻⁴ in the demixing model and +2.38×10⁻⁴ in active recruitment; at m=1.0, the corresponding medians were −2.86×10⁻³ and −1.27×10⁻³. These values are dimensionless, calculated without force-measurement noise in this first challenge, and are not estimates of piconewton forces. Their sign change reflects the imposed saturating actin-response law, not a demonstrated reversal of microglial force production.

### Spectral separation is defeated by a different active operator

The initial contrast result motivated a new rule based on the difference S=g₃−g₂ between the growth rates of two spatial modes in the first 15 s. In the linearized demixing model, a band of modes grows with a non-monotonic dependence on wavelength; the two initial recruitment models have different specified dispersions. The criterion S>0 was frozen before a second, independently seeded synthetic challenge. In that set, 200/200 demixing cases and 0/200 cases from each original recruitment family were assigned the demixing label at each m (Supplementary Figure 5). A further force-sign criterion with independently simulated force noise retained 193/200 and 200/200 demixing cases at m=0.3 and 1.0, respectively, and did not reduce the already zero false-positive counts in the two original recruitment families. The force readout therefore did not improve their separation in this design.

A third, newly reserved challenge tested a phenomenological active redistribution model without the imposed passive demixing free energy, whose linear spatial dispersion was constructed to match that of the demixing model but whose nonlinear dynamics differ. The unchanged spectral criterion labeled 200/200 of these active-lookalike cases as demixing at each concentration. Adding the force-sign criterion still labeled 191/200 and 200/200 active-lookalike cases as demixing at m=0.3 and 1.0, respectively. The 95% Wilson intervals for 191/200 and 200/200 are 91.7–97.6% and 98.1–100%, respectively. Consequently, the spectral result resolves the three initially specified families but does not identify the mechanism when this active spatial alternative is admitted. The active lookalike is an illustrative phenomenological law, not a measured microglial process; its full nonequilibrium phase behavior was not established.

### A nonlinear amplitude response separates the two specified operators

The shared linear dispersion of the demixing model and the active lookalike does not require their nonlinear responses to be identical. After the spectral result, we specified a new perturbation consisting of three amplitudes of the same sinusoidal profile and reserved new synthetic parameter parents. For a mode with discrete Laplacian eigenvalue λ₂, the passive demixing law with a cubic free-energy coefficient β predicts a leading attenuation of the fundamental proportional to \(3M\beta\lambda_2A^3/4\). Its third-harmonic source is proportional to \(M\beta\lambda_6A^3/4\). The ratio of these two normalized coefficients is therefore \(Q=\lambda_6/(3\lambda_2)\), approximately 2.92 on the selected grid. For the specified active lookalike, the corresponding leading ratio is 1/3. These are model-specific weak-amplitude consequences of the assumed spatial operators, not an established universal signature of phase separation. The active cubic coefficient was chosen to match the passive third-harmonic source, so the comparison required both the fundamental and third harmonic.

A fixed Q>1.2 rule was evaluated using noisy molecular profiles at 0 and 5 s after each of three imposed amplitudes. In fresh independent synthetic cases, it assigned the demixing label to 199/200 passive cases at m=0.3 and 200/200 at m=1.0; it assigned the same label to 0/200 active-lookalike cases in each regime (Supplementary Figure 6). The 95% Wilson intervals for passive recovery were 97.2–99.9% and 98.1–100%; the upper interval limit for 0/200 false positives was 1.9% in each regime. The passive quartic coefficient varied over the prespecified range 0.7–1.3. At m=0.3, median Q was 2.23 for the demixing model and 0.239 for the active lookalike; the corresponding values at m=1.0 were 2.19 and 0.237. The force outputs at the largest amplitude were also recorded, but were not used in this classifier and were not assigned a measurement-noise model in this challenge.

### A change in molecular accessibility can propagate to actin and displacement

To connect the molecular premise to the actin–mechanical model, we introduced two coarse conformational macrostates, accessible and inaccessible, with accessible fraction q. For an illustrative fixed-temperature ensemble with state degeneracies ΩA and ΩI and energy difference ΔE=EA−EI, the equilibrium fraction is \(q_{\rm eq}=[1+\exp(-z)]^{-1}\), where \(z=\log(\Omega_A/\Omega_I)-\Delta E/(k_BT)+u\). We modeled relaxation toward this target with a positive effective time constant and used q to multiply the common actin-assembly response. At a fixed input, the corresponding two-state transition rates satisfy the specified equilibrium ratio; the imposed time-dependent input drives the gate, and the one-way cascade does not close a chemical-work or heat balance. The fraction q is not itself conformational entropy. If microstates are equiprobable within each macrostate, the illustrative entropy is \(S/k_B=-q\log q-(1-q)\log(1-q)+q\log\Omega_A+(1-q)\log\Omega_I\). This idealized expression includes within-state degeneracy and population mixing; it is not an entropy measured for an actual protein.

The model has an exact identification boundary: adding the same dimensionless offset to the assumed log-degeneracy ratio and energy gap leaves z, q and all downstream outputs unchanged. For fixed ΩI, this transformation changes the assumed entropy by \(k_B q\delta\) while leaving the predicted motion unchanged. Conversely, an increase in the log-degeneracy ratio at fixed energy gap shifts the equilibrium fraction, but the same shift in z can be produced by lowering the energy gap. Even perfect measurements of q under these conditions cannot independently recover the entropic and energetic terms. Conversely, a shift in q can arise from several molecular processes, and our effective gate is not a resolved N-WASP structural ensemble.

We next compared fast and slow gate relaxation with identical equilibrium populations, common recruitment and the same downstream equations. The challenge and classifiers were specified before generating 200 independent synthetic test parents per concentration regime. With position sampled every 60 assigned seconds and the prespecified observation noise, position-only classification was 334/400 (83.5%) at m=0.3 and 397/400 (99.25%) at m=1.0 (Supplementary Figure 7). Adding hypothetical direct q observations during the pulse increased classification to 399/400 (99.75%) and 400/400 (100%), respectively. Parent-resampled 95% intervals for the paired gains were 13.0–19.5 and 0–1.75 percentage points. The higher-concentration result shows that 60-s sampling does not automatically erase a fast-gate distinction under the assumed response and noise. It does not establish that either gate occurs in a cell.

The equal-rate control underlines that forced labels are not evidence of two mechanisms. At m=0.3, when both synthetic classes used the fast gate, the position-plus-q classifier labeled all 200 cases in each arbitrarily named group fast; the same occurred at m=1.0. This shows strong assignment to one trained class, not an ability to recognize that a relevant comparator is absent. The class fractions and bootstrap intervals describe the specified artificial distributions, not biological accuracy or a cellular false-positive rate.

### Negative feedback through a dynamical intermediate can destabilize the modeled steady state

The original joint-observation cascade is feed-forward. For any two states driven by the same prescribed inputs and fixed positive relaxation times, their difference follows a constant triangular linear system whose eigenvalues are \(-1/\tau_q,-1/\tau_a,-1/\tau_x,-1/\tau_s,-1/\tau_l\). All initial-state differences therefore decay asymptotically; polynomial transients can occur when rates coincide. With constant input, this particular model has a single attracting equilibrium; bounded periodic inputs yield an attracting periodic response. Aperiodic forcing can still generate an aperiodic output, and none of these statements constrains feedback, noise or chaos in microglia. In one assigned example with time constants 8, 25, 20, 4 and 5, the largest conditional exponent was −0.04 per model time unit.

To ask what physical ingredient could change this conclusion, we closed a hypothetical delayed negative-feedback loop: \(\dot q=\operatorname{sigmoid}(z_0-gx)-q\), \(\dot a=q-a\) and \(\dot x=a-x\), with equal unit relaxation times. Here x is an effective mechanical feedback coordinate, not a measured traction, and \(z_0=\log(\Omega_A/\Omega_I)-\Delta E/(k_BT)+u\) retains the illustrative ensemble bias. At equilibrium, \(q_*=a_*=x_*\); defining \(K=gq_*(1-q_*)\), the characteristic equation is \((\lambda+1)^3+K=0\), with a linear stability boundary at K=8. With g=40 and q*=0.1 (z₀≈1.803), K=3.6 and the largest eigenvalue real part was −0.234. At q*=0.5 (z₀=20), K=10 and it was +0.0772, with a complex-conjugate unstable pair. Thus, within this assumed circuit, a shift in ensemble bias can move the equilibrium across an oscillatory linear instability. This does not show a stable limit cycle, deterministic chaos, cellular feedback or a change in measured conformational entropy. Because the same z₀ can be produced by different energy and degeneracy offsets, mechanical observations cannot separately identify these molecular terms without independent structural constraints.

Conformational entropy describes the distribution of molecular microstates; dynamical entropy concerns the information generated by trajectories; thermodynamic entropy production concerns dissipation. These quantities are not interchangeable. In particular, broad conformational heterogeneity can coexist with a contracting macroscopic response. The stronger hypothesis is that an independently manipulated molecular ensemble changes a mechanically coupled protrusion's dynamical regime. A null or unchanged response to a validated molecular perturbation, within a prespecified equivalence margin and with demonstrated assay sensitivity, would bound that coupling in the tested context; an unchanged macroscopic trace alone would not rule out hidden compensation.

### Existing microglial measurements sample distinct steps of the response

Published studies on microglia probe different levels of the response. Li and colleagues reported ABI3-positive puncta in microglia in 5×FAD mouse tissue and in HMC3 cells [10]. Their fusion and fission examples use HEK293T cells. Puncta and fluorescence recovery can motivate a test of molecular organization, while identification of a phase transition requires measurements that distinguish it from active redistribution. Endogenous MYO1F and ASAP1 colocalize with F-actin-rich structures in fixed primary mouse and human iPSC-derived microglia [11], establishing spatial association.

Kessels and colleagues imaged F-actin at 6-s intervals in cultured primary mouse microglia [12]. Möller and colleagues acquired membrane and F-actin images at 15-s intervals in a representative zebrafish engulfment series [13]. These recordings resolve actin dynamics in their respective preparations. The nonlinear amplitude test used here requires imposed molecular amplitudes and a measurement of the early molecular response over a 5-s window.

Primary rat microglial traction measurements complement actin and molecular imaging by estimating stress transmitted to a substrate [14]. The traction measurements and actin recordings cited here are from separate preparations. A test of the coupled response requires synchronized measurements with an independent calibration of the mechanical readout.

### Available protein reporters motivate the comparison of the two memory mechanisms

Published optical reporters provide candidate observations outside the present simulator. Ward and colleagues developed the N-WASP FRET reporter Stinger and recorded reporter responses in living-cell systems, including HEK293 and COS-7 preparations [15]. The construct includes an N-terminal truncation, and its optical response is not automatically the accessible fraction q of our coarse model. In macrophages, Cammer and colleagues used a WASP FRET sensor and a conformation-sensitive antibody after CSF-1 stimulation [16]. The reported time-point images include fixed cells, so their time course must not be treated as a longitudinal recording of the same cell. Neither study measures the proposed microglial q–actin–force trajectory.

The associated [HMC3 imaging protocol and RhoA FRET dataset](https://doi.org/10.17632/y6z2zmsx4f.1) illustrate reporter acquisition in live microglial cells [20]. Testing the proposed model requires measurements of N-WASP accessibility, actin and mechanics in the same preparation.

In a separate macrophage study, Dovas and colleagues reported similar WT and Y291F WASP biosensor activity within podosomes, with a different spatial distribution outside podosomes; in a separate actin-FLIP assay, Y291E-expressing cells had longer GFP-actin fluorescence-loss half-lives than Y291F-expressing cells [17]. The comparisons are WT versus Y291F for the FRET readout and Y291E versus Y291F for FLIP, in different assays. Similar FRET does not establish equivalence, and FLIP reflects exchange and transport as well as binding, geometry and bleaching; it is not a direct estimate of the actin relaxation parameter. These observations motivate distinct molecular and actin measurements but do not demonstrate matched conformational states with different actin memory in the same cell, nor establish either mechanism in microglia.

The N-WASP–Nck work includes chemical-shift assignments for a recombinant construct [6]. Those ensemble-averaged measurements provide molecular context. A separate time-resolved experiment is needed to estimate accessibility kinetics and relate them to actin assembly.

We additionally screened published mechanical-sensor reference values in the Amiri source workbook. In U2OS cells, the median FRET efficiencies in the reported distributions were 22.218% for intact CTS control sensor (53 values), 21.660% for lysed TS (9 values) and 19.626% for lysed CTS (6 values). The lysate-minus-intact-CTS median differences were −0.558 and −2.592 percentage points. The supplementary legend describes cells and measurements at different times, but the workbook values do not provide verified parent identifiers for each row. These are descriptive distribution differences, not independent-cell uncertainty intervals or evidence that the reference assay failed. They also cannot establish a quantitative relationship between molecular tension and substrate traction, nor calibrate the N-WASP sensor in microglia.

### Synthetic optical calibration

A separate optical reference experiment tested an idealized calibration rather than the biological instrument. We simulated single-component standards with true two-channel transfer matrix [[1, 0.15], [0.25, 1]], known background and known source intensities. From 60 synthetic standards per component, the fitted matrix was [[0.998, 0.151], [0.250, 0.996]]. For 100 independently generated mixed-source standards, median absolute relative error in the recovered acceptor-to-donor source ratio decreased from 21.1% without correction to 3.95% after matrix inversion. These are conditional performance summaries for an assumed linear instrument, not measured camera calibration or FRET efficiency. A matrix fitted from single-color standards would not by itself identify q–actin state mixing, the time response of a biosensor, or direct biological effects of either intervention.

### Gate–actin–mechanical model and paired public observations

The two-state gate used k₀=0.5, b=0.2 and α=0.4; assembly used kₚ=1, k_d=0.4 and ρ=0.5; mechanics used Fₚ=k=1 and ζ=2; and the sensor proxy used E₀=0.26, g=0.08 and τₛ=1. All values and times are assigned and dimensionless. Autonomous input was zero; the periodic input was 0.4 sin(2πt/30), and the stochastic input was an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with stationary standard deviation 0.4, correlation time 4 and seed 202611180401. RK4 used a 0.02-unit step; the periodic input was evaluated at the step midpoint, and each stochastic input value was held constant over a step before an exact Ornstein–Uhlenbeck transition on that grid. The 400-unit run discarded the first 200 units and retained samples every 2 units. Halving the deterministic periodic step changed saved states by at most 5.15×10⁻⁷. State bounds and finite values were checked throughout.

The autonomous equilibrium was calculated by an independent scalar root of the steady assembly equation, with q=sigmoid(b−αFₚa) and x=Fₚa/k; its residual was 8.3×10⁻¹⁷. For the selected parameters and the invariant domain, the weighted infinity-norm logarithmic matrix measure is at most −0.05, with state weights (0.3,0.9,1,1); hence trajectories receiving the same prescribed input contract. This is a sufficient condition for the specific parameter set, not a generic property of every molecular–mechanical network. The analysis does not provide a full thermodynamic balance or experimental parameter inference.

We extracted three representative paired FRET efficiency and traction traces from Figure 2 of Amiri et al. [7]. Original sampling times were used, including a 30-min gap in one trace. Endpoint differences and within-cell Kendall associations describe the individual trajectories. The data were used for comparison with published measurements, independently of the synthetic model parameters.

### Autonomous feedback benchmark and Lyapunov estimates

The autonomous four-state system in Results was specified after the earlier feed-forward model proved contracting under a common prescribed input. The coordinate maps q=σ(U/20), a=σ(V/30) and m=σ((W−25)/30) are smooth one-to-one transformations for finite U,V,W; their coefficients and the displacement relaxation time are assumed. A smooth coordinate transformation preserves asymptotic Lyapunov exponents when its Jacobian and inverse remain bounded along the trajectories; it does not imply that finite-time exponents calculated from a single noisy observable are unchanged. The chosen mappings impose neither chemical conservation nor a physical force balance, and the r–ensemble relationship is not calibrated. Different splits of the control parameter into baseline, energy and degeneracy produce identical trajectories.

We integrated state and full four-dimensional variational equations by fourth-order Runge–Kutta with step 0.01, and repeated the reference modified system with step 0.005. After a 200-unit transient, estimates accumulated over 1,000 assigned units with QR reorthonormalization every 0.1 unit. Three specified initial conditions assessed sensitivity to initial state for η=0.5, r=28; a canonical η=0, r=28 case and a lower-parameter η=0.5, r=10 case served as comparators. We verified bounded finite traces, 0<x<1, and the equality between the estimated sum of exponents and mean Jacobian trace within the declared tolerance. Ten consecutive 100-unit blocks were recorded as convergence diagnostics, not independent replicates. An independently coded DOP853 solver used relative tolerance 10⁻⁹, absolute tolerance 10⁻¹¹, maximum step 0.1 and a 0.5-unit QR interval for the reference modified system. Individual chaotic trajectories need not coincide pointwise over long times. The observed numerical agreement supports this constructed regime, not a general proof of attractor existence, a biological time scale or an exhaustive search over model parameters.

### Assessment of available microglial data

We compared published molecular-organization, actin and traction studies of microglia [10–14] by preparation, reporter, interval and perturbation. This comparison identifies which measurements can constrain the model and which measurements would be required to estimate the response to a calibrated molecular redistribution.

### Proposed independent molecular-field study

The public actin and membrane series can guide an exploratory assessment of geometry and measurement requirements after channel, timing and sample provenance are verified. They cannot serve as the reserved molecular amplitude test. A calibration experiment would measure a defined regulator, spatial geometry and exchange, test a reproducible set of calibrated redistribution amplitudes including a sham, and estimate the acquisition transfer function and biological variation. Actin and calibrated mechanical measurements would be complementary, rather than inputs to Q. The present 1D periodic and conserved-field assumptions would require direct assessment; a boundary-adapted or open-domain model would constitute a new test.

A proposed estimator would fit the complex fundamental and phase-aligned third-mode trajectories jointly, conditioning on their initial values and additional observed modes. For example, the contribution of a nonzero initial third mode evolves even in the absence of nonlinear forcing; therefore its raw finite-time change is not by itself an instantaneous cubic source. Joint uncertainty in the two coefficients should be retained, especially when the fundamental attenuation is compatible with zero and the ratio is poorly resolved. Simulations using a pilot-informed observation model and independent synthetic realizations would be needed to establish recovery and error rates under the actual imaging conditions. Neither the assigned 5-s window nor the synthetic Q>1.2 threshold is transferable without that calibration.

After calibration, model classes, preprocessing, exclusions, estimands, decision rule and sample size would be specified before examining a separate set of animals, donors or independent preparations, retaining cells and repeated perturbations within their parent unit. A held-out result could favor one of the declared models, remain compatible with both, or be inconsistent with both. Biological samples do not carry known model labels; synthetic or independently validated controls are required to estimate class-specific error rates. This design is a proposal, not an experiment performed here, and even a favorable result would not exclude all active dynamics or by itself establish phase separation.

To distinguish the N-WASP gate from local concentration and actin memory, a separate pilot should acquire a validated conformational reporter, independently calibrated local N-WASP abundance, actin assembly or turnover, and deformation or traction at the same cellular region and compatible times. A full-length N-WASP FRET reporter has been demonstrated in carcinoma cells ([Lorenz et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2004.04.008)); its function, expression and optical behavior require validation in microglia. A randomized pulse–washout–rechallenge protocol would compare distinct histories followed by the same final stimulus, alongside distinct candidate gate and actin-remodeling manipulations and sham controls. A history effect after measured concentration and sensor signal recover would be compatible with memory, but does not by itself locate it in actin; an independently verified actin manipulation is needed. Comparing gate states at matched local concentration and history asks a controlled mechanistic question, but matching on a variable affected by treatment can introduce bias; any causal interpretation requires a declared estimand, experimental support for overlap, cross-talk measurements and an explicit causal model. Assay controls should establish sensor function, donor/acceptor cross-talk, density-dependent intermolecular FRET, bleaching, registration, substrate calibration and a relaxed traction reference. Pilot estimates would set the timing, matching uncertainty, discrimination thresholds and sample size before entire preparations, animals or donors are held out. No such matched experimental triple was identified or acquired in this study.

### Coarse conformational ensemble and reserved kinetic challenge

We specified a dimensionless two-macrostate model with accessible population q, input \(u(t)\), assumed log-degeneracy ratio σ and energy difference ε in units of \(k_BT\). At fixed temperature \(q_{\rm eq}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\sigma-\epsilon+u)\), and \(\dot q=(q_{\rm eq}-q)/\tau_q\). The target is instantaneous equilibrium for the illustrative fixed-input system, not an assertion that the driven population is at equilibrium. With \(H=[h(m(1-r))+h(m(1+r))]/2\), the effective actin and mechanical equations were \(\dot b=(qH-b)/\tau_b\) and \(\dot x=(b-x)/\tau_x\); p and r followed the finite recruitment equations below. The gate input was one and a half dimensionless units from 20 to 80 assigned seconds, while the shared recruitment input acted from 20 to 200. All parameters and the input are synthetic.

For each m, 200 development and 200 disjoint test parents supplied shared recruitment and mechanical parameters, and distinct fast (5–10 s) or slow (35–70 s) assumed gate time constants. For m=1.0, we evaluated the fixed classifier on a new test set with random streams independent of those used for development. An earlier evaluation shared a seed with the development calculation and was excluded from the independent comparison. A binary pooled-covariance nearest-centroid classifier was fitted only on development parents, separately for position and position plus idealized early q; Gaussian noise and covariance regularization were prescribed before the test. Accuracy gains were bootstrapped over paired parents, not the 400 class trajectories independently. Identical-rate nulls were generated with independent observation errors. Integration used a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme with a 0.25-s step, with input discontinuities aligned to step boundaries. Bounds were checked at every step; for one nominal parent, halving the step changed any state by at most 1.76 × 10⁻⁹. The exact entropy–energy offset symmetry was preserved to numerical precision. These local checks do not establish general convergence, full thermodynamic consistency or biological identification.

For the post hoc filter twin, we set the organization response to a constant and evaluated the matched initial-value problems with matrix exponentials. The result illustrates the commutative transfer function of the gate and actin filters; it is not an independent estimate of either time constant.

### Model and inputs

Two equal-area compartments had concentrations c±=m(1±r), with fixed dimensionless mean m and 0≤r≤1. We used m=0.3 and 1.0 as two illustrative regimes. An external input u(t) was one from 20 to 200 s and zero otherwise. An effective signalling variable p and accessibility a followed

\[
\dot p=(u-p)/\tau_p,\qquad \dot a=(0.5+0.4p-a)/\tau_a.
\]

These variables are phenomenological; p is not a measured phosphorylation occupancy and a is not a measured structural ensemble. For uniform regulation, r=0. Finite recruitment used

\[
\dot r=-(1-r^2)(r-Rp)/\tau_r,
\]

while the demixing analogue used

\[
\dot r=-(1-r^2)r(r^2-R^2p)/(\tau_rR^2).
\]

At fixed p, both dynamics descend their stated convex or non-convex potentials with nonnegative mobility 1−r². The second form is a symmetry-breaking analogue only. Its two compartments conserve their total regulator by construction, but do not represent a resolved phase boundary. For both nonuniform descriptions the initial contrast was 0.03; the uniform class used zero. With h(c)=c²/(1+c²), H=[h(c−)+h(c+)]/2, the common downstream equations were

\[
\dot b=(aH-b)/\tau_b,\qquad \dot x=(b-x)/\tau_x.
\]

Here b is an effective actin-dependent load and x is dimensionless deformation. With positive τx, the mechanical equation is consistent with an overdamped spring–drag balance in normalized units; the chemical work required to maintain b is not explicitly budgeted. The initial b and x were set to their corresponding pre-input steady values.

### Synthetic training and independent testing

For each parent, τp, τa, τr, τb and τx were drawn uniformly from 6–12, 5–12, 8–20, 10–25 and 12–35 s, respectively; R was drawn uniformly from 0.45–0.75. Parameter values were identical across the three candidate classes within a parent and independent across parents. These ranges were chosen as synthetic test conditions, not inferred distributions. The three-class development and test sets had 300 independent parents per concentration each, with distinct random seeds. A further 300 parents per concentration were reserved for a driven finite-recruitment alternative and another 300 for a null coupling control.

The forward system was integrated to 300 s using midpoint RK2 with a 0.5-s step. Position was sampled at 0, 60, 120, 180, 240 and 300 s; independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.005 was added to each dimensionless position measurement. The optional contrast channel was sampled at the same times with independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.03. Position features were five differences from the first sample; contrast features were the six contrast observations. The noise levels and all model parameters were assumed rather than measured. This sampling model does not reproduce projection, uncertain intra-stack timing or the full imaging process.

Separate regularized pooled-covariance nearest-centroid classifiers were trained for each concentration and channel. Feature scaling and covariance were estimated only from the development set. Performance was calculated on the held-out parents without changing the model, features or classifier. The 5,000-replicate bootstrap resampled parent indices and retained all paired classes and channels within each index. This procedure characterizes finite synthetic-sample variability, not uncertainty about which biological model is correct.

### Spatial perturbation and reserved challenges

The spatial domain was a periodic one-dimensional interval of length 32 in dimensionless units, sampled at 64 grid points. A zero-mean order parameter φ specified the positive dimensionless regulator concentration c=m(1+0.6φ), with fixed mean m=0.3 or 1.0. Each synthetic parent supplied the same initial profile to all model classes: modes 2, 3 and 4 with independently drawn phases and a common initial spatial standard deviation drawn from 0.025–0.035. These profiles represent an idealized imposed redistribution, not a measured microglial perturbation. The time unit was assigned one second for comparison with imaging cadence; no kinetic rate was biologically calibrated.

The demixing class obeyed

\[
\partial_t\phi=M\partial_{xx}(\phi^3-\phi-\kappa\partial_{xx}\phi),
\]

with energy \(E=\int[(\phi^2-1)^2/4+\kappa(\partial_x\phi)^2/2]dx\). The passive recruitment comparator followed \(\partial_t\phi=D\partial_{xx}\phi\). A first active alternative followed \(\partial_t\phi=D_A\partial_{xx}\phi+\lambda[\phi-\phi^3-\langle\phi-\phi^3\rangle]\). The last term is a globally balanced phenomenological source and does not define a resolved molecular reaction network. M, κ, D, D_A and λ were drawn over the predeclared ranges in the study protocol. The three fields used the same downstream readouts \(\partial_tb=(0.7h(c)-b)/\tau_b\), \(F=\langle b\rangle\) and \(\dot x=(F-x)/\tau_x\). Thus F is an effective dimensionless actin-associated load; its coupling to mechanics is imposed and no dimensional force calibration or mechanochemical feedback is included.

For the first challenge, 200 independently drawn parents per concentration were paired across the three models. Independent Gaussian field noise with standard deviation 0.002 was added at each grid point. G₆₀ was the measured spatial standard deviation at 60 s divided by that at time zero; the predetermined binary rule was G₆₀>1.3. A second challenge was designed after observing the first result and used fresh parameter parents and errors. For initial modes 2 and 3, \(g_n=\log(|\widehat\phi_n(15)|/|\widehat\phi_n(0)|)/15\) and S=g₃−g₂; S>0 was the frozen positive rule. For a joint outcome, a noisy force change was also required to have the sign specified for the given m; independent Gaussian force noise had standard deviation 0.0002 at both 0 and 60 s. The sign rule assumes m is known.

After this spectral challenge, a third challenge used another independent set of 200 parents per concentration and an active-lookalike operator

\[
\partial_t\phi=-M\partial_{xx}\phi-M\kappa\partial_{xxxx}\phi-\gamma[\phi^3-\langle\phi^3\rangle],
\]

where γ was drawn uniformly from 0.05–0.15 in assigned reciprocal seconds. This model has the same linear dispersion as the demixing model but different nonlinear dynamics; its global cubic source is phenomenological, its energy supply is not modeled, and its full nonequilibrium phase behavior was not established. No classifier or threshold was re-estimated on its outcomes. The two subsequent challenges were prospectively reserved within the simulation sequence but were formulated after results from earlier challenges; none is an independent experiment on cells.

The spatial solver used periodic finite-difference Laplacian eigenvalues with spectral stepping, a 0.25-s step and implicit treatment of the highest-order term. At fixed parameters, the discrete demixing energy was checked at every step. Mass and concentration positivity were checked for all simulated cases. For one reference per original class, reducing the step to 0.125 s and increasing the grid to 128 points changed φ at sampled times by at most 0.00336 and 0.00923, respectively, against the prespecified 0.02 tolerance; the corresponding largest force or deformation differences were 9.36×10⁻⁶ and 6.22×10⁻⁶ against a 0.002 tolerance. The separate active-lookalike reference passed the same checks (maximum φ changes 0.00313 and 0.00872). These are local numerical comparisons, not a convergence proof over all parameter values. A small-amplitude Fourier-mode check agreed with the semidiscrete linear growth rates within 2.57×10⁻⁴ per assigned second. Wilson intervals characterize finite synthetic classification counts; zero observed false positives do not establish a zero population error rate.

### Reserved nonlinear amplitude challenge

After observing the active-lookalike spectral challenge, we froze a further test using fresh independent parents and noise. Each parent supplied the same parameters and phase to both operators and to three sinusoidal perturbations with amplitudes A=0.12, 0.28 and 0.40 at spatial mode 2. The passive model used a cubic coefficient β drawn uniformly from 0.7–1.3. In the active lookalike, the cubic source coefficient was set to \(\gamma=M\beta\lambda_6\), matching the leading passive third-harmonic source. This is a constructed hard comparator, not an inferred molecular mechanism. We estimated fundamental growth from noisy fields at 0 and 5 s, fit its dependence on A² across all three amplitudes and used the negative slope as \(\widehat C_1\). Phase-aligned third-harmonic change at A=0.40, divided by \(-5A^3\), gave \(\widehat C_3\). The prespecified classification was \(\widehat C_1>0\) and \(Q=\widehat C_3/\widehat C_1>1.2\). The perturbation phase and amplitudes were assumed known; independent molecular field noise had standard deviation 0.002 at each observation. The effective force at 5 s was recorded without noise and excluded from the classification.

There were 200 independent parameter parents per concentration, shared across model classes and amplitudes. At each amplitude, field noise was independent across classes and observation times. Three amplitudes from one parent are not three independent replicates. The independent dataset used the same spatial domain and grid as the earlier challenges, with a 0.05-s time step. One reference per class was recomputed at 0.025 s and on 128 grid points; the largest field difference was 9.55×10⁻⁴ and the largest force difference 8.50×10⁻⁷. All cases preserved positive effective concentration and the specified total-regulator tolerance; the assumed passive discrete energy did not increase. Separately, direct real-space finite differences recovered both leading coefficient ratios independently of the time-stepping implementation. These checks do not establish global numerical convergence or biological validity.

### Numerical checks and matched control

The analytical two-compartment identity, positivity and bounds of the computed states, and equal null position trajectories were checked. For six representative trajectories, repeating the integration at a 0.25-s step changed contrast, actin output or deformation at the 60-s samples by at most 4.90 × 10⁻⁵ dimensionless units, below the prespecified 10⁻³ comparison tolerance. The step-size comparison assesses numerical sensitivity for these six trajectories.

The matched-recruitment calculation was specified after observing the in-family challenge. It used the nominal demixing trajectory to construct the target s(t), interpolated the target between stored times and integrated the convex recruitment equation independently. Its agreement is an illustration of the analytical equivalence under the stated assumptions, not an independent blinded recovery experiment.

### Optical reference and feedback stability checks

For the separate idealized calibration, 60 donor-only and 60 acceptor-only synthetic references had known source intensities drawn between 500 and 3,000 and an assumed known background of 40. Poisson count means were generated from the declared 2×2 matrix. Least squares estimated its columns, and 100 independent mixed-source synthetic standards evaluated ratio recovery after matrix inversion. These references were not extracted from biological images. The instrument response is linear by construction and sensor cross-reactivity, delay and bleaching are not estimated by this experiment.

For common prescribed inputs, subtraction of the original cascade trajectories produces a constant triangular difference system. Its eigenvalues give the conditional asymptotic rates, including when the gate's sigmoid is nonlinear in the prescribed inputs; repeated roots can add polynomial transients. A matrix-exponential calculation verified one assigned example. For the proposed feedback closure, differentiating the sigmoid at equilibrium gives gain K, and the Jacobian determinant gives \((\lambda+1)^3+K\). We compared analytical real parts with direct eigenvalues for two fixed parameter cases. These local linear calculations make no assertion about attractors beyond the equilibrium's local stability, chemical work, real mechanical constitutive relations or a molecularly calibrated protein ensemble.
