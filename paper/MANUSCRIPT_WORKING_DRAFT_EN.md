# Conformational and actin memory can leave identical signatures in protrusion models

## Abstract

When a protrusion keeps responding after a stimulus, the persistence could reside in the accessibility of an actin regulator or in the actin network it helped assemble. We construct two mechanochemical models that exchange the gate and actin relaxation times. The gate follows different histories in the two models, yet a transformation of the gate coordinate, input couplings and observation map preserves the predicted mixed fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), actin and deformation trajectories for arbitrary common inputs. In a separate simulation, a fixed classifier distinguished two restricted kinetic families in 400/400 cases under one pulse order. After reversal it assigned all cases to one family, scoring 200/400. With known reporter gain and an exact actin reference, a gate-sensitive readout can distinguish the pair by amplitude when its response to actin is bounded and sampling resolves the gap relative to noise. For an actin-sensitivity bound of 0.20, a reporter time constant of 10 assigned seconds and Gaussian noise with SD 0.03 in normalized units, the probability of a correct declaration fell from 99.94% at 15-s sampling to 78.52% at 60-s sampling under the least informative pulse order and alignment of samples to pulses. Increasing the bound to 0.50 lowered the 15-s probability to 67.57%. The models identify what a prospective test of N-WASP accessibility, actin persistence and mechanics in microglial protrusions would need to calibrate.

## Introduction

When a protrusion continues to advance after a stimulus subsides, its motion reveals persistence. In a microglial process, an actin regulator might stay accessible after its upstream drive subsides, or it might reset rapidly while the actin network it helped assemble reorganizes slowly. Both histories can sustain an edge response. We call this persistence *memory* in the kinetic sense of a slow relaxation after a change in input. The experimental question is whether measurements from the same protrusion can locate that slow step.

N-WASP connects molecular accessibility to assembly of branched actin. Relief of autoinhibition exposes the machinery that activates Arp2/3, and membrane recruitment changes the amount of N-WASP available locally. Nck-mediated activation, membrane curvature and prewetting illustrate how accessibility and local concentration can affect assembly through different routes [1–6]. Conformational disorder can broaden the ensemble sampled by a regulatory region [5,25]. The time the regulator spends in an accessible state and the time the protrusion takes to recover are separate quantities. The models therefore distinguish the fraction of N-WASP available for activation from the amount of N-WASP and from the state of the actin network.

The measurements sample different steps of that sequence. FRET depends on fluorophore geometry and on how the reporter responds to its molecular environment. An actin reporter samples a labeled pool whose intensity can reflect assembly, binding or turnover. Tip displacement reflects network activity together with mechanical resistance and coupling to the surroundings. Traction microscopy estimates stress transmitted to a substrate by fitting a mechanical model to its displacement [7,14]. To interpret these signals together, we need a model of how each arises.

An unpublished proteomic dataset from CD11b-enriched mouse brain provides context for focusing on N-WASP [26]. We use assigned kinetic parameters to ask whether the same joint observations can result from a slow gate or from slow actin remodeling. In a separate simulation, we test whether a classifier trained under one pulse order transfers when the order changes. The ambiguity motivates an independently calibrated reporter and a paired test in microglia. Two feedback models then distinguish loss of steady-state stability from autonomous chaotic behavior.

## Methods

### Exact equivalence of the two linear models

We represent conformational accessibility, actin assembly and deformation by the dimensionless deviations \(q,a,x\) from a common resting state. Each state relaxes toward a moving target. In model A, a molecular input \(u\) moves the target for accessibility, an assembly input \(v\) enters the actin balance, and deformation follows the assembly state. With time in assigned seconds and zero initial deviations,

\[
8\dot q=u-q,\qquad 25\dot a=q+v-a,\qquad 20\dot x=a-x.
\]

The optical precursor is \(f=\rho q+(1-\rho)a\), where \(\rho=8/25=0.32\). This choice makes the ambiguity exact. Set \(q'=f\). Multiplying its derivative by 25 and substituting the first two balances gives \(25\dot q'=u-q+(1-\rho)(q+v-a)=u+(1-\rho)v-q'\). The identity \(q'-a=\rho(q-a)\) then yields a second realization,

\[
25\dot q'=u+(1-\rho)v-q',\qquad 8\dot a=q'+\rho v-a,\qquad 20\dot x=a-x.
\]

Model B reports \(f'=q'\). The transformation exchanges the molecular and actin relaxation times while preserving both the assembly state that drives deformation and the optical precursor. It is invertible because \(\rho>0\). With corresponding initial states the two realizations therefore agree on \((f,a,x)\) for every common input history. They differ in where \(v\) enters, a distinction that matters if its molecular selectivity is known independently. The 20-s deformation law is an effective relaxation of shape driven by assembly. Its 20-s time constant is assigned. For the nominal comparison, within the respective 20–80-s and 140–200-s windows, the gate pulse had amplitude 0.30 and the actin pulse amplitude 0.20. The augmented linear system was propagated at 1-s intervals by the matrix exponential for each constant input segment.

### Joint observation and synthetic perturbation challenge

The exact construction allows the two models to share observations. We next asked how well a classifier could identify the slower process when we restricted the reporter mixing and the pathways by which inputs affect states. Within each independent parameter draw, the two kinetic families exchanged one fast time (uniform on 6–12 s) and one slow time (30–50 s) between accessibility and assembly. They shared a deformation time (12–30 s) and reporter times (2–6 s each). All five states started at 0.5. For the accessibility reporter state \(s\) and actin reporter state \(l\), the balances were

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tau_q\dot q&=(1+e^{-(u+\beta v)})^{-1}-q, &
\tau_a\dot a&=q+\alpha u+0.20v-a,\\
\tau_x\dot x&=a+\chi v-x, &
\tau_s\dot s&=q-s,\qquad \tau_l\dot l=a-l .
\end{aligned}
\]

The unit inputs occupied [20,80) and [140,200) s, respectively. Four conditions comprised sham, either pulse alone, and both pulses in sequence. Uniform cross-effects allowed \(u\) to enter the assembly balance (\(|\alpha|\le0.03\)) and \(v\) to enter the accessibility and deformation balances (\(|\beta|\le0.20, |\chi|\le0.03\)). The model assigns no molecular target to these interventions.

To distinguish biochemical mixing from optical contamination, we generated photon counts after reporter relaxation. Write \(E=0.45-0.20\tanh[2(s-0.5)]\) for the effective transfer efficiency and \(G_i(t)=g_i N\exp(-b_i t)\) for the gain and bleaching factor of channel \(i\). Expected donor, acceptor and actin counts were

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_D &=40+G_D(1-E),\\
\mu_A &=40+G_A[E+c(1-E)+kl],\\
\mu_L &=40+G_L(l+jE).
\end{aligned}
\]

Each parent used a common photon scale \(N\) uniformly drawn from 1,500 to 3,000. For each channel, gain \(g_i\) was uniform on [0.85, 1.15] and bleaching rate \(b_i\) was uniform on [0, 0.00025] per assigned second. Here \(c\le0.04\) represents donor leakage and \(k,j\le0.03\) represent cross-channel contributions, all nonnegative. Each count comprised a Poisson draw plus independent Gaussian read noise (SD 2 counts). Let \(D_i,A_i,L_i\) be the noisy donor, acceptor and actin counts at time \(i\). We used \(R_i=(A_i-40)/\max(D_i-40,1)\) and \(L'_i=(L_i-40)/\max(L_0-40,1)-1\). The FRET features were \(R_i-R_0\). The deformation channel carried independent additive Gaussian noise (SD 0.005 in normalized shape units) and its features were differences from the noisy baseline. The joint vector contained 16 post-baseline optical values per reporter at 20, 35, 50, 65, 80, 95, 110, 140, 155, 170, 185, 200, 215, 230, 260 and 300 s. The five post-baseline deformation samples were taken at 60-s intervals. Within each channel, using the same baseline for all features can induce dependence.

Development and reserved test sets contained 300 and 200 independent parameter parents, respectively. The two kinetic families and four input conditions remained grouped within a parent; development and test used separate random streams. The sham and bootstrap calculations also used independent streams. For each non-sham condition and channel set, we standardized features using development means and standard deviations and assigned each trace to the nearest family centroid by squared Mahalanobis distance. The common covariance was the sum of the within-family residual outer products divided by the number of development traces, plus \(0.1 I\). The primary contrast was the gain in accuracy of the joint over the deformation-only classifier under both inputs. The 95% interval used 5,000 percentile bootstrap resamples of paired parameter sets. We used Wilson intervals for the within-family classification proportions.

For the sham detector, we standardized the FRET feature at each time against the first family of the development sham set, using its sample standard deviation. The score was the maximum absolute standardized deviation over times. The 95th percentile of the development sham scores was used as a strict exceedance threshold on the first family of the independent sham set and on both families under both inputs. Sham false-declaration proportions were summarized with Wilson intervals. In the equal-time control, the gate and actin times were both set to the geometric mean of the paired fast and slow times. The classifier still assigned each of the two copies to a family, but neither copy had a true fast or slow gate label. The independently specified linear comparison was fixed before seeing challenge outcomes and checked with exact matrix exponentials. RK4 used a 0.25-s step with input boundaries aligned to steps; a nominal step-halving comparison changed states by at most 2.27 × 10⁻⁸. State bounds and nonnegative photon means were checked, and no denominator floors were encountered. These are local numerical and synthetic checks, not calibration of the microscope, an exhaustive model comparison or a cellular validation.

### Pulse reversal and a readout of molecular accessibility

We used the joint classifier fitted to the same 300 development parameter sets, keeping the features, covariance regularization and sampling times fixed. An independent sample of 200 parameter sets supplied both family assignments and four conditions per set. For the fixed joint and deformation-only classifiers, we compared \(u\) followed by \(v\) with \(v\) followed by \(u\), keeping the pulse windows at [20,80) and [140,200) s. The classifier and its time coordinates were unchanged. A wider combined stress simultaneously set α in [−0.12,0.12], β in [−0.6,0.6], χ in [−0.12,0.12], channel bleed in [0,0.20] and other optical cross-channel terms in [0,0.15], and drove the sensor filter with 0.5q+0.5a. The same parents were retained across conditions; noise draws were condition-specific. We calculated equal-weight family accuracy and parent-paired bootstrap intervals from 5,000 resamples. This design tests the frozen, time-indexed rule, rather than information-theoretic identifiability under all estimators. States and intensities were checked for finiteness and nonnegativity; no ratio denominator was floored.

For the independent readout design, we propagated the linear twin using a matrix exponential at 1-s intervals from 0 to 300 s with pulse amplitudes of 0.3 for u and 0.2 for v, both pulse orders and zero initial deviations. We evaluated sensor response times of 0, 10 and 30 assigned seconds (zero denotes instantaneous response), cadences of 5, 15 and 60 s with all integer phases, and bounds \(b=0.20,0.50,0.68\). Let \(H\) apply the unit-gain first-order reporter filter and then sample at the specified times. Reporter gain and the relative scale of the modeled states are assumed known, and the common filtered actin template \(Ha\) is subtracted without error. The Gaussian variance per sample is held constant across acquisition schedules. For a reporter with actin sensitivity \(m\), \(0\le m\le b\), subtraction of \(Ha\) leaves \(y=H[(1-m)(q-a)]+\epsilon\). Write \(g=H(q_A-a)\). Since \(q_B-a=\rho(q_A-a)\), the two possible mean traces are \(c_Ag\) and \(c_Bg\), with \(c_A\in[1-b,1]\) and \(c_B\in[\rho(1-b),\rho]\). We compare the closest rival means over all sensitivities compatible with the same calibration bound. Both models predict the same template shape and differ only in its coefficient. Under independent Gaussian noise of SD \(\sigma=0.03\) per sample, the scalar projection \(g^\top y/(\sigma\lVert g\rVert)\) has unit variance. For the bounds tested, the nearest means are separated by

\[
d=(1-b-\rho)\lVert g\rVert/\sigma.
\]

We used the smallest template norm over both pulse orders and all integer phases at each cadence. After centering the scalar at the nearest model-B mean, the two nearest means are 0 and \(d\). These correspond to \(m_B=0\) and \(m_A=b\). With \(t=\max(d/2,1.95996)\), we declared A above \(t\), B below \(d-t\), and abstained between them. At either nearest mean, correct and wrong declaration probabilities are \(\Phi(d-t)\) and \(\Phi(-t)\), respectively, where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function. The abstention probability is \(1-\Phi(d-t)-\Phi(-t)\). At touching means, the total probability of either declaration is \(2\Phi(-1.95996)\), approximately 0.05. We checked these probabilities with 10,000 independent Gaussian realizations per family and setting (seed 202611180301). Adaptive integration split at pulse boundaries independently agreed with the matrix-exponential filtered contrast to \(1.61\times10^{-11}\) in the original order at \(\tau=10\) s. The sensitivity estimates apply to the specified synthetic signal, noise and acquisition times. An experimental power calculation would require measured reporter kinetics and noise.

### Optical unmixing with independent source standards

For the separate synthetic reference calibration, we represented the two recorded intensities by \(\mathbf y=M\mathbf w+\mathbf b\), where \(\mathbf w\) contains the source intensities and \(\mathbf b\) is the known background. The synthetic instrument had unit diagonal entries, \(M_{12}=0.15\) and \(M_{21}=0.25\). We fitted each column of \(M\) on 60 single-component standards at known source intensities drawn uniformly between 500 and 3,000 counts, using least squares with a fixed background of 40 counts and Poisson photon noise. For each of 100 independent mixed standards, the two source intensities were drawn independently from the same 500–3,000 count range. We compared the background-subtracted channel ratio \((y_2-40)/(y_1-40)\) with the unmixed ratio \(\widehat w_2/\widehat w_1\), using

\[
\widehat{\mathbf w}=\widehat M^{-1}(\mathbf y-\mathbf b),\qquad
R=w_2/w_1.
\]

The endpoint was the median of \(\lvert\widehat R/R-1\rvert\). The calibration estimates photon-channel mixing given specified sources. The coefficient \(m\) instead specifies how the molecular reporter responds to a cellular actin state within the observation model.

### Molecular availability and feedback dynamics

The stationary model distinguishes local N-WASP amount \(Pp\) from the accessible fraction \(q\), where \(P\) is total amount and \(p\) is the fraction at the tip. The normalized active pool is \(z=Ppq/(VK)\), with local reaction volume \(V\) and concentration scale \(K\). At fixed \(z\), the dimensionless actin equilibrium \(a_*\) solves
\[
\frac{z}{1+z}(1-a_*)(1+0.5a_*)-0.4a_*=0,
\qquad F_*=a_* .
\]
We set \(P=V=K=1\). The two examples choose different accessibility biases and local amounts while fixing \(z=0.25\). Both therefore use the same actin balance. \(F_*\) is an effective load in the model.

To test whether a returning mechanical signal can destabilize a steady state, we added negative feedback to a cascade with equal relaxation times. All times in this calculation are in assigned model units. With \(\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}\), ensemble bias \(z_0\), and mechanical inhibition \(g\), its equations are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot q&=\sigma(z_0-gx)-q,\\
\dot a&=q-a,\qquad \dot x=a-x.
\end{aligned}
\]

At equilibrium \(q_*=a_*=x_*\). At fixed \(g\), the ensemble bias \(z_0\) sets the equilibrium accessibility; the slope of the sigmoid there gives \(K=gq_*(1-q_*)\). The linearized loop has characteristic equation \((\lambda+1)^3+K=0\). For \(K\ge0\) its leading real part is \(-1+K^{1/3}/2\). This eigenvalue tests local stability. It does not specify the long-time dynamics beyond the instability.

A separate Lorenz-derived circuit illustrates an internal source of irregular dynamics. Its signed states \(U,V,W\) are assigned to conformation, actin regulation and internal feedback. Here \(V\) denotes a dynamic coordinate. The displacement proxy \(x\) follows \(W\) through a one-way filter, so it cannot act back on the core. The equations are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot U&=10(V-U)+\eta\tanh(U/10),\\
\dot V&=U(r-W)-V,\\
\dot W&=UV-\tfrac83 W,\\
\dot x&=[\sigma((W-25)/30)-x]/2 .
\end{aligned}
\]

We compared \(\eta=0.5\) at \(r=10\) and \(r=28\). Bounded proxies \(q=\sigma(U/20)\) and \(m_{\mathrm{fb}}=\sigma((W-25)/30)\) display the state projection. We integrated the full-state variational equation with periodic QR orthonormalization over 1,000 assigned time units after a transient of 200 units, estimating each exponent from the cumulative logarithm of the corresponding diagonal stretch divided by the accumulation time. The Supporting Information records the alternative initial conditions, time-step check, independent adaptive integrator and separate analytical limit for the canonical Lorenz system. These calculations test dynamical feasibility in this circuit.

## Results

### Slow conformational and actin relaxation can produce identical signals

The exact construction exchanges the molecular and actin relaxation times under common inputs (Figure 1, panel a). The states \(q\) and \(a\) are deviations from baseline accessibility and effective actin assembly. In model A they relax over 8 and 25 assigned seconds, respectively. Model B exchanges the times and changes how the input enters the gate and actin balances. The new gate coordinate is \(q'=0.32q+0.68a\), which coincides with the mixed FRET precursor \(f\) of model A. For the nominal molecular and actin inputs \(u\) and \(v\), the gate histories differed by up to 0.126 in normalized state units (Figure 1b). The mixed FRET, actin and deformation predictions overlay (Figure 1c). The maximum numerical discrepancies were 2.23 × 10⁻¹⁶, 1.67 × 10⁻¹⁶ and 8.33 × 10⁻¹⁷, respectively. An independent exact-rational substitution verified the identity for arbitrary common \(u\) and \(v\). Applying the same downstream optical map and noise distribution preserves equality of the predicted observation distributions. The 68% mixing of the underlying states and the associated input coupling are features of the constructed pair, outside the ranges of the classification study below. This construction identifies an ambiguity when the reporter and input couplings are allowed to differ between models.

The restricted synthetic comparison asks which channel carries information about the slower kinetic step under a specified protocol. For each parameter set we exchanged the gate and actin relaxation times while holding the other parameters common across families. We tested gate-directed and actin-directed pulses alone, both pulses and sham. The inputs could affect more than one state. The generator produced donor, acceptor and actin-reporter photon counts and dimensionless deformation, with assigned sensor delays, bleaching, limited optical mixing and measurement noise.

The most informative readout depended on which input was applied (Figure 1d). With \(u\) alone, 398/400 cases (99.5%) were classified correctly from FRET and 399/400 (99.75%) from all three channels. The corresponding values were 208/400 (52.0%) for deformation and 191/400 (47.75%) for the actin reporter. FRET resolved 199/200 cases in each family, with a 95% Wilson interval of 97.2–99.9% for either count. With \(v\) alone, deformation, FRET, actin and the joint readout classified 389/400 (97.25%), 203/400 (50.75%), 400/400 and 400/400 correctly, respectively. Under both inputs, the joint readout improved accuracy from 398/400 (99.5%) for deformation to 400/400 (100%). The gain was 0.5 percentage points, with a paired bootstrap interval of 0–1.25 points. Each condition contributed two model outcomes for each of 200 independent synthetic parameter sets. In this restricted family, deformation can carry information about a downstream time constant after an actin-directed input. The inferred class still depends on the observation model.

A separate detector asked whether the FRET trace changed enough to exceed a threshold calibrated on training shams. It labeled 15/200 independent shams positive (7.5%, 95% Wilson interval 4.6–12.0%) and detected 199/200 and 159/200 cases in the two families under both inputs. The interval is conditional on the learned threshold and describes performance in this simulation. In a different control, the gate and actin time constants were equal. Even with no true kinetic ordering, the classifier assigned the fast label to 129/200 and 135/200 cases in the two arbitrarily labeled copies. The rule was forced to choose a class, so those counts describe that choice in an equal-time control.

### Reversing the pulses sends every case to the same class

The classifier assigned every case to the fast-gate family after pulse reversal (Figure 1e). We tested the existing classifier on 200 new parameter sets, each simulated under both kinetic families. In the original order, gate-directed followed by actin-directed stimulation, it classified all 400 cases correctly. In the reversed order it classified 200/400 correctly (50%) because it assigned all 200 slow-gate cases to the fast-gate family. Accuracy fell by 50 percentage points, with a paired bootstrap interval of −50 to −50 points. Every parameter set contributed the same paired change, producing a zero-width interval for this cohort. The deformation-only rule also assigned every reversed case to the fast-gate class. The reversal shows how much this fixed, time-indexed classifier relied on its development schedule. The information available to a classifier trained across schedules remains an open question.

With the original pulse order, the joint classifier still assigned 400/400 cases correctly when we widened the input cross-effects and optical mixing together and made the FRET-state filter respond equally to \(q\) and actin. Deformation alone classified 319/400 (79.75%) correctly. Under the same combined changes, pulse reversal led both classifiers to call every case fast. The effects of the individual changes cannot be separated in this comparison. The synthetic input labels specify intended targets, whose cellular selectivity would have to be established independently.

### Reporter specificity and sampling set the limits of discrimination

An additional readout can distinguish the exact pair if it is sensitive enough to the regulator's accessibility (Figure 2, panel a). We let the response contain an actin fraction \(m\): \(z=h_\tau*[(1-m)q+ma]+\epsilon\), with a unit-gain first-order reporter kernel \(h_\tau\). Independent calibration would impose \(0\le m\le b\), and each rival can take a different value within that bound. After subtracting the common filtered actin trace, the expected signal is a multiple of \(h_\tau*(q_A-a)\). The allowed coefficients range from \(1-b\) to 1 for model A and from \(0.32(1-b)\) to 0.32 for model B. Their ranges meet at \(b=0.68\) and overlap above it (Figure 2b). Below 0.68 the closest means differ in amplitude on the same time course. Filtering and sampling determine how much of that difference the recording captures. This bound is specific to the modeled pair and to the assumed common scale and known gain.

With the same noise per sample, wider sampling intervals left fewer observations to resolve the difference in amplitude. For a single synthetic trace with a reporter time constant of 10 assigned seconds, Gaussian noise of SD 0.03 in normalized units and \(b=0.20\), the probability of a correct declaration was 99.94% at 15-s sampling and 78.52% at 60-s sampling (Figure 2c). These values use the closest allowed means and the least informative pulse order and integer sampling phase at each cadence. Increasing \(b\) to 0.50 reduced the 15-s probability to 67.57%. At a fixed 60-s cadence, a slower reporter also reduced correct declarations (Figure 2d). At 15 s, most of the lost correct calls became abstentions as the actin bound increased (Figure 2e). At the touching means (\(b=0.68\)), 5.30% of 10,000 independent simulated noise draws yielded either declaration, against the analytical 5% rate at the same overlap. These probabilities assume calibrated gain, an exact actin template and the same independent noise per sample at each cadence. Noise in the actin channel, correlations from subtraction and cadence-dependent photon counts can change the detectable contrast.

Correcting the photon ratio addresses a different problem. In 100 withheld synthetic mixtures, the median absolute relative error fell from 21.1% to 3.95% after optical unmixing with a matrix estimated from independent standards (Figure 2f). The true off-diagonal values were 0.15 and 0.25, and the estimates were 0.151 and 0.250, respectively. The standards recover channel mixing for the specified linear instrument. Estimating the reporter's response to actin \(m\), its kinetics and intervention cross-effects requires observations of the underlying cellular states.

### Local abundance can offset accessibility, and feedback can destabilize the steady state

The schematic separates local abundance, accessibility, actin and mechanics (Figure 3, panel a). In the stationary product law, twice as much local N-WASP can offset half the accessible fraction. With unit reaction volume, the two combinations \((p,q)=(0.5,0.5)\) and \((1,0.25)\) both give \(z=0.25\) (Figure 3b). The model then gives the same actin state and load, 0.3723, for both. The two stationary outputs coincide at fixed volume. Resolving the persistence of accessibility therefore requires a separate measure of local amount. Figure 3a also illustrates a possible return path from mechanics to the regulator. We analyze its dynamical consequences in separate models.

When two feed-forward trajectories receive the same input, their differences decay. Closing the negative feedback loop changes that behavior near the equilibrium. For equal time constants and \(g=40\), the two ensemble biases place the equilibrium at accessibility 0.10 and 0.50, giving \(K=3.6\) and \(K=10\), respectively. The leading eigenvalue real part crosses zero, changing from −0.234 to +0.0772 per assigned time unit (Figure 3c). The unstable roots are a complex pair. This crossing establishes an oscillatory linear instability of the chosen equilibrium. The crossing specifies local stability in this equal-time loop.

A separate Lorenz-derived circuit illustrates how an autonomous system can remain irregular under constant conditions (Figure 3d). At \(\eta=0.5\) and \(r=28\), the gate–feedback projection revisits regions of its state space, and nearby full-state trajectories have a largest finite-time Lyapunov estimate of 0.9036 per assigned time unit. The independent adaptive integrator gives 0.9076. At \(r=10\), the trajectory approaches a fixed point and the largest full-state estimate is −0.5097. These finite-time estimates support chaotic dynamics in the constructed internal circuit. The displacement proxy inherits the irregularity through the one-way filter. The local instability in Figure 3c and the numerical example in Figure 3d concern different equations. In the Lorenz-derived model, perturbations confined to the output filter decay at rate \(1/2\) in model time.

## Discussion

### Why changing the pulse sequence tests the classifier

The exact pair exposes an ambiguity in the observation map. When the input couplings and reporter sensitivity change together as specified, the gate history can change while all three observed signals remain the same for any pulse sequence. The reversal experiment tests a different question: whether a classifier trained within a narrower family can still identify the slower step under a new sequence. After pulse reversal, the 50% accuracy came from assigning every case to one family. For the exact pair, the original signals have the same distribution, so no classifier using those signals can outperform chance with equal class proportions. The ambiguity can be resolved by a readout or perturbation that constrains a quantity the two models predict differently. Independently establishing that an input couples selectively to a given state would provide such a constraint.

### Which response persists in a microglial protrusion?

After a calibrated perturbation of N-WASP accessibility, the two models assign different histories to the regulator while actin and deformation follow the same trajectories. With local N-WASP amount accounted for and the common actin reference removed, the slow-actin model predicts the larger residual amplitude on the same time course when the calibrated ranges are disjoint. Testing this contrast in cells requires estimates of reporter kinetics and intervention selectivity. The decision rule can leave a response unassigned even when the models predict disjoint ranges of mean amplitudes.

Published actin studies sampled at intervals of 6 and 15 s in different preparations [12,13]. A primary rat microglial traction study recorded substrate beads at 30-s intervals [14]. An experiment that combines multiple reporters and mechanics must establish its own timing, including the delay between channels.

A calibration experiment would follow local N-WASP amount, accessibility, actin turnover and mechanics in the same protrusion. Single-source optical and bleaching controls [19] characterize the instrument. Separate controls would estimate reporter gain, response time, actin sensitivity, intervention cross-effects and noise. An accessibility-locked reporter exposed to an actin perturbation is one possible control for an actin-dependent signal at fixed accessibility. Actin turnover and substrate traction address different alternatives: prolonged motion after accessibility recovers could reflect persistence in the network or a change in how it transmits load. Traction would be estimated with a relaxed substrate reference and independent characterization of substrate mechanics. The pulse schedule and decision rule would be set on calibration preparations before testing new preparations, with cells and regions nested within each preparation.

### What irregular motion reveals about its source

Conformational disorder can change which molecular states are available to a regulator. Testing whether disorder drives irregular motion through feedback requires locating the persistent state and establishing a return path from mechanics to the regulator. Fluctuating inputs, actin remodeling and mechanical feedback can each make a trajectory irregular [21,22]. A feed-forward circuit can follow an aperiodic input while still forgetting differences in its initial state. The inhibitory loop shows how an ensemble bias can cross a local stability boundary. The separate Lorenz-derived construction illustrates how irregular internal dynamics can reach a mechanical output. Testing a return path in a cell would require an independently characterized mechanical perturbation and a calibrated upstream readout.

## Conclusion

Identical FRET, actin and deformation trajectories can arise when two models assign slow relaxation to different processes. For the pair studied here, independent calibration narrows the predicted response ranges of a gate-sensitive reporter. Its response time and the acquisition interval determine how much of the resulting contrast can be observed. This gives a concrete way to test where a microglial protrusion retains the effect of a stimulus.

### A constructed feedback circuit shows numerical evidence of autonomous chaos

Differences in the feed-forward gate–actin–mechanics cascade decay when trajectories receive the same prescribed input. To examine autonomous chaos, we used a separate circuit with reciprocal coupling. Its dimensionless states \(U\), \(V\) and \(W\) are an effective signed conformational coordinate, an actin-regulatory deviation and a mechanical feedback coordinate. A filtered state \(x\) represents normalized displacement. With constant coefficients, the circuit evolves without time-varying or stochastic input according to

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot U&=10(V-U)+\eta\tanh(U/10),\\
\dot V&=U(r-W)-V,\\
\dot W&=UV-\tfrac83 W,\\
\dot x&=[\sigma((W-25)/30)-x]/2.
\end{aligned}
\]

The bounded maps \(q=\sigma(U/20)\), \(a=\sigma(V/30)\) and \(m=\sigma((W-25)/30)\) display the gate, actin and feedback coordinates. The signed states and their mapped values are dimensionless. At \(\eta=0\), the three-state core is the classical Lorenz system [8]. Setting \(\eta=0.5\) adds a bounded self-feedback term to that core. The signed interactions inherited from the Lorenz core have no molecular or mechanical derivation here.

With \(\eta=0.5\) and \(r=28\), the largest full-state finite-time Lyapunov exponent was 0.9036 per assigned time unit at the reference initial condition and 0.9068 and 0.9011 at two others (Supplementary Figure 2). In each case the second exponent was close to zero and the remaining two were negative. The reference exponents were approximately (0.9036, −0.0004, −0.5083, −14.5280). Their sum differed from the time-averaged Jacobian trace by about 0.0001. Halving the Runge–Kutta step gave a largest exponent of 0.9051, and an independently implemented adaptive DOP853 solver gave 0.9076. At \(r=10\) and \(\eta=0.5\), the trajectory approached a fixed point and the largest estimate was −0.5097. These finite numerical estimates characterize the stated parameters and initial states. The extent of the chaotic regime in parameter space remains unresolved.

For every complete trajectory on that compact invariant set, the linear filter for \(x\) has a unique bounded history, given by a convergent exponentially weighted integral. Even an ideal displacement measurement leaves this pair unresolved. The equivalence is an algebraic consequence of the stated equations.

At fixed \(r\), shifting the bracket by \(d\) and \(r_0\) by \(-\kappa d\) leaves every trajectory unchanged. The assignments of the signed and bilinear terms to gate, actin and mechanical states do not follow from mass-action chemistry. ### Early accessibility readings can distinguish model kinetics that deformation misses

We represented the molecular gate by two coarse conformational macrostates, accessible and inaccessible. At fixed temperature the equilibrium accessible fraction is \(q_{\rm eq}=[1+\exp(-z)]^{-1}\), with \(z=\log(\Omega_A/\Omega_I)-\Delta E/(k_BT)+u\). Here \(\Omega_A\) and \(\Omega_I\) are the state degeneracies, and \(\Delta E=E_A-E_I\) is their energy difference. The accessible fraction \(q\) relaxes toward this target with a positive effective time constant and multiplies the common actin-assembly response. For fixed input, the two-state rates satisfy the specified equilibrium ratio. Under time-dependent driving, the population can lag its target, and the cascade leaves the work and heat balance unspecified. If microstates are equiprobable within each macrostate, the dimensionless entropy is \(S/k_B=-q\log q-(1-q)\log(1-q)+q\log\Omega_A+(1-q)\log\Omega_I\). Its terms distinguish population mixing from within-state degeneracy. It is an illustrative ensemble expression; \(q\) alone is an occupancy.

Adding the same dimensionless offset \(\delta\) to the log-degeneracy ratio and energy gap preserves \(z\), \(q\) and the downstream trajectories. With \(\Omega_I\) fixed, this changes the assumed entropy by \(k_B q\delta\) while leaving motion unchanged. Increasing the log-degeneracy ratio at fixed energy and lowering the energy gap at fixed degeneracy can therefore shift \(q_{\rm eq}\) in the same way. Even an ideal \(q\) measurement leaves the energetic and entropic contributions unresolved under this model. The gate describes coarse accessibility without resolving structural states of N-WASP.

### Negative feedback can destabilize the modeled steady state

In the original feed-forward cascade, two trajectories driven by the same prescribed inputs converge for any fixed positive relaxation times. Their difference obeys a constant triangular system with eigenvalues \(-1/\tau_j\) for \(j\in\{q,a,x,s,l\}\). Initial-state differences decay asymptotically, although coincident rates can add polynomial transients. Constant input gives a single attracting equilibrium, and bounded periodic input gives an attracting periodic response. Aperiodic input can still yield aperiodic output. In the assigned example with time constants 8, 25, 20, 4 and 5, the largest conditional exponent was −0.04 per model time unit. These properties follow from the one-way connections and common drive in this cascade.

We next asked whether a returning mechanical signal can destabilize the steady state. The hypothetical delayed negative-feedback loop obeys \(\dot q=\operatorname{sigmoid}(z_0-gx)-q\), \(\dot a=q-a\) and \(\dot x=a-x\), with equal unit relaxation times. The mechanical feedback coordinate \(x\) is an effective model state. The input bias remains \(z_0=\log(\Omega_A/\Omega_I)-\Delta E/(k_BT)+u\). At equilibrium \(q_*=a_*=x_*\), and the linearized loop gain is \(K=gq_*(1-q_*)\). The characteristic equation \((\lambda+1)^3+K=0\) puts the stability boundary at \(K=8\). With \(g=40\) and \(q_*=0.1\) (\(z_0\approx1.803\)), \(K=3.6\) and the largest eigenvalue real part was −0.234. At \(q_*=0.5\) (\(z_0=20\)), \(K=10\) and it was +0.0772. The unstable eigenvalues formed a complex-conjugate pair. A shift in ensemble bias thus crosses an oscillatory linear instability in this circuit. The stability result concerns the equal-time sigmoid loop. Energy and degeneracy offsets can produce the same \(z_0\), so resolving their contributions requires independent structural information.

Conformational entropy concerns the distribution of molecular microstates, dynamical entropy concerns information generated by trajectories, and thermodynamic entropy production concerns dissipation. A broad conformational ensemble can coexist with a contracting macroscopic response. The stronger hypothesis is that changing the molecular ensemble independently can change the dynamical regime of a mechanically coupled protrusion. With a validated perturbation and demonstrated assay sensitivity, a response within a prespecified equivalence margin would bound that coupling in the tested context. The same macroscopic trace can still conceal compensating changes.

Endogenous MYO1F and ASAP1 also colocalized with F-actin-rich structures in fixed primary mouse microglia and human iPSC-derived microglia [11], establishing a spatial association.

Kessels and colleagues imaged F-actin every 6 s in cultured primary mouse microglia [12]. In a representative zebrafish engulfment series, Möller and colleagues acquired membrane and F-actin images every 15 s [13]. Each recording resolves actin dynamics in its own preparation. The modeled amplitude test addresses an additional observable, the molecular response in the first 5 s following imposed redistribution of a defined regulator.

Traction measurements in primary rat microglia estimate the stress transmitted to the substrate [14]. Those recordings and the cited actin series come from separate preparations. Estimating the coupled response requires synchronizing the molecular and actin signals with an independently calibrated mechanical readout.

### Existing reporters motivate paired tests of N-WASP and actin memory

Ward and colleagues recorded living-cell responses of the N-WASP FRET reporter Stinger, including in HEK293 and COS-7 preparations [15]. This construct has an N-terminal truncation, and mapping its optical response to the coarse accessible fraction \(q\) requires independent evidence. In macrophages stimulated with CSF-1, Cammer and colleagues used a WASP FRET sensor and a conformation-sensitive antibody [16]. Some time-point images were obtained from fixed cells, which provide a comparison across cells sampled at different times. An accessibility–actin–force trajectory in an individual microglial protrusion remains to be measured.

A published [HMC3 imaging protocol and RhoA FRET dataset](https://doi.org/10.17632/y6z2zmsx4f.1) illustrate reporter acquisition in live microglial cells [20]. Testing the proposed gate model calls for paired measurements of N-WASP accessibility, actin and mechanics in the same preparation.

In macrophage podosomes, Dovas and colleagues reported similar WASP biosensor activity for WT and Y291F, with a different spatial distribution outside podosomes [17]. In a separate actin-FLIP assay, GFP-actin fluorescence-loss half-lives were longer in Y291E-expressing cells than in Y291F-expressing cells. The FRET and FLIP assays thus used distinct perturbation contrasts. Similar FRET leaves the degree of equivalence unresolved, and the FLIP half-life depends on exchange, transport, binding, geometry and bleaching. Together the assays motivate separate molecular and actin measurements. They leave unresolved whether matched conformational states can accompany different actin memory in a single cell or in microglia.

The N-WASP–Nck study provides chemical-shift assignments for a recombinant construct [6]. These ensemble-averaged measurements give structural context. Estimating accessibility kinetics and their relation to actin assembly requires time-resolved measurements.

We also summarized the published U2OS reference distributions in the Amiri workbook. Median FRET efficiencies were 22.218% for intact CTS control sensor (53 values), 21.660% for lysed TS (9 values) and 19.626% for lysed CTS (6 values). The lysate medians were 0.558 and 2.592 percentage points lower than the intact CTS median, respectively. The legend describes cells and measurements at different times, and the workbook lacks verified identifiers linking each row to its parent cell. The medians therefore describe the recorded values. They leave cell-level uncertainty, assay performance, the molecular tension–traction relationship and the calibration of a microglial N-WASP reporter unresolved.

### Single-source standards improve ratio recovery in a simulated detector

We simulated single-component standards for a two-channel detector with transfer matrix [[1, 0.15], [0.25, 1]], known background and known source intensities. Sixty standards per component gave a fitted matrix of [[0.998, 0.151], [0.250, 0.996]]. In 100 independently generated mixed-source standards, inversion reduced the median absolute relative error in the acceptor-to-donor source ratio from 21.1% to 3.95%. This result quantifies ratio recovery for the assumed linear detector. A physical instrument would require its own calibration. Sensitivity to both gate and actin states, biosensor kinetics and direct effects of the interventions on biological states require separate experiments.

### Gate, actin and load model and paired public recordings

For the two-state gate we used \(k_0=0.5\), \(b=0.2\) and \(\alpha=0.4\). Assembly used \(k_p=1\), \(k_d=0.4\) and \(\rho=0.5\). Mechanics used \(F_p=k=1\) and \(\zeta=2\), and the sensor proxy used \(E_0=0.26\), \(g=0.08\) and \(\tau_s=1\). All parameters and times are assigned and dimensionless. The autonomous input was zero and the periodic input was \(0.4\sin(2\pi t/30)\). The stochastic input was an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with stationary standard deviation 0.4, correlation time 4 and seed 202611180401. We integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) at a 0.02-unit step. We evaluated the periodic input at the step midpoint and held each stochastic input value constant over a step before applying an exact Ornstein–Uhlenbeck transition on that grid. Of the 400 time units, we discarded the first 200 and retained a sample every 2 units thereafter. Halving the periodic time step changed saved states by at most 5.15×10⁻⁷. We checked the state bounds and finiteness throughout.

To recover the steady state independently of time stepping, we solved the assembly equation as a scalar root in \(a\), substituting \(q=\operatorname{sigmoid}(b-\alpha F_p a)\) and \(x=F_p a/k\). The residual was \(8.3\times10^{-17}\). To bound the rate at which trajectories forget their initial state, we used a weighted infinity-norm logarithmic matrix measure. For the selected parameters and invariant domain, it is at most −0.05 with weights (0.3,0.9,1,1). This bound suffices for contraction under a common prescribed input in this system. The model uses assigned parameters and leaves the full thermodynamic balance open.

Separately, we extracted three representative paired FRET-efficiency and traction traces from Figure 2 of Amiri et al. [7]. We retained the original sampling times, including the 30-min gap in one trace. First-to-last changes and within-cell Kendall associations describe each trajectory. These published measurements provided context, independently of the chosen model parameters.

### Autonomous feedback circuit and full-state Lyapunov estimates

We specified the autonomous four-state circuit after finding that the earlier feed-forward model contracts under common prescribed input. The proxy maps \(q=\sigma(U/20)\), \(a=\sigma(V/30)\) and \(m=\sigma((W-25)/30)\) are smooth and one-to-one for finite \(U,V,W\). Their coefficients and the displacement relaxation time are assigned. A smooth change of coordinates preserves asymptotic Lyapunov exponents if its Jacobian and the inverse remain bounded along the trajectories. Finite-time estimates based on a single noisy observable need not have this property. The maps impose neither chemical conservation nor a physical force balance. The link between \(r\) and the ensemble is uncalibrated, and different decompositions into baseline coupling, energy and degeneracy give identical trajectories.

We integrated the state and full four-dimensional variational equations by RK4 with step 0.01 and repeated the reference modified system with step 0.005. After a 200-unit transient we accumulated the estimates over 1,000 assigned units, with QR reorthonormalization every 0.1 unit. Three initial conditions tested sensitivity to starting state at \(\eta=0.5,\ r=28\). The canonical case \(\eta=0,\ r=28\) and the lower-\(r\) case \(\eta=0.5,\ r=10\) served as comparators. We checked that the traces stayed finite and bounded, that \(0<x<1\), and that the sum of exponents matched the mean Jacobian trace within the stated tolerance. We retained ten consecutive 100-unit blocks to assess convergence over the same trajectory. An independently coded DOP853 solver checked the modified reference system with relative tolerance 10⁻⁹, absolute tolerance 10⁻¹¹, maximum step 0.1 and a 0.5-unit QR interval. Divergence of numerical trajectories point by point at long times can coexist with agreement in the exponent estimates. These estimates characterize the constructed regime for the stated starting states and parameters. They do not establish attractor existence for the modified system or a biological time scale.

### Proposed calibration and independent microglial tests

The public actin and membrane series can help define geometry and measurement requirements once the channels, timing and sample provenance are verified. A future amplitude test would directly observe a named regulator, spatial geometry and exchange after a reproducible set of calibrated redistribution amplitudes and a sham. This calibration would estimate the acquisition transfer function and variation across biological units. Actin and calibrated mechanics would provide complementary observations. The estimator \(Q\) uses the molecular field. The planned test would also check whether the assumed periodic, conserved one-dimensional field is adequate. Changing to an open or boundary-adapted domain would define a new test.

An estimator for the experiment would jointly fit the complex Fourier coefficient of the imposed mode and the phase-aligned third mode, conditioning on their starting values and additional observed modes. A third mode already present at the start can evolve even in the absence of nonlinear forcing, so its finite-time change alone cannot estimate the instantaneous cubic source. In particular, when fundamental attenuation is compatible with zero, the ratio can be poorly constrained. The two coefficient estimates should retain their joint uncertainty. Independent synthetic realizations from a pilot-informed observation model would quantify recovery and error rates under the observed imaging conditions. The 5-s sampling window and \(Q>1.2\) threshold refer to the specified simulation. Their applicability to a cellular assay depends on calibration.

After calibration, the proposed study would fix model classes, preprocessing, exclusions, estimands, decision rule and sample size before examining a separate set of animals, donors or independent preparations. Cells and repeated perturbations would remain nested within those units. Reserved observations could favor one declared model, remain compatible with both or conflict with both. Because the true biological mechanism is unknown, class-specific error rates would require synthetic or independently validated controls. Even a favorable test would support separation from the specified rivals only.

A separate pilot to locate N-WASP versus actin memory would pair a validated conformation-sensitive reporter, independently calibrated local N-WASP abundance, actin assembly or turnover, and deformation or traction in the same cellular region at compatible times. A full-length N-WASP FRET reporter has been demonstrated in carcinoma cells ([Lorenz et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2004.04.008)). Its expression, function and optical behavior require validation in microglia. A randomized pulse–washout–rechallenge protocol would compare different histories followed by the same final stimulus, using distinct candidate gate and actin perturbations and sham controls. Persistence after measured local abundance and sensor signal recover is compatible with memory elsewhere in the system. Locating that memory in actin requires an independently verified actin perturbation. Matching gate states for local abundance and history defines a controlled comparison. If treatment changes the matching variable, the match itself can bias the estimate. Causal interpretation would require a specified estimand, experimental overlap, measurements of cross-effects and an explicit causal model. Calibration would address sensor function, donor–acceptor cross-talk, density-dependent intermolecular FRET, bleaching, registration, substrate response and a relaxed traction reference. Pilot data would set timing, matching uncertainty, discrimination thresholds and sample size before holding out whole preparations, animals or donors. We did not identify or acquire a matched conformational, actin and mechanical series in microglia. Both studies described here are proposals.

### Conformational gating and the independent kinetic test

The dimensionless model gives the accessible population \(q\) a fixed-temperature target set by the input \(u(t)\), assumed log-degeneracy ratio \(\sigma\) and energy gap \(\epsilon\) in units of \(k_BT\). Its target and dynamics are \(q_{\rm eq}=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\sigma-\epsilon+u)\) and \(\dot q=(q_{\rm eq}-q)/\tau_q\). The target is the equilibrium for the instantaneous input held fixed. A time-varying input can change faster than the population relaxes. With \(H=[h(m(1-r))+h(m(1+r))]/2\), the effective actin and mechanical equations are \(\dot b=(qH-b)/\tau_b\) and \(\dot x=(b-x)/\tau_x\). The variables \(p\) and \(r\) follow the finite-recruitment equations below. The gate input was 1.5 from 20 to 80 assigned seconds. The shared recruitment input was one from 20 to 200, and both inputs were zero outside their respective windows. The initial signaling state and contrast were zero, \(q(0)=0.5\) and \(b(0)=x(0)=0.5h(m)\). Signal, recruitment, actin and deformation times were independently sampled from uniform intervals of 6–12, 8–20, 10–25 and 12–35 s. The recruitment coefficient was uniform on [0.45, 0.75].

At each \(m\), 200 development parameter sets and 200 disjoint test sets supplied shared recruitment and mechanical parameters, and distinct assumed gate times of 5–10 or 35–70 s. At \(m=1.0\) we tested the fixed classifier with random streams independent of development. We excluded an earlier evaluation that shared a development seed. Position sampled at 0, 60, 120, 180, 240 and 300 s received independent Gaussian noise (SD 0.005), and we subtracted the first noisy sample from subsequent samples. The two additional gate observations at 35 and 65 s had independent Gaussian noise (SD 0.03). For each readout, the development data fitted a nearest-centroid classifier with standardized features and pooled within-family covariance regularized by \(0.01 I\). We bootstrapped individual parameter sets, retaining both class trajectories in each resample. Identical-rate controls used independent observation errors. We integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at step 0.25 s, aligning the discontinuities in input with step boundaries. We checked the bounds at every step and verified the entropy–energy offset symmetry to numerical precision. Halving the step for one nominal parameter set changed any state by at most 1.76 × 10⁻⁹. These checks cover the implemented cases and leave general convergence, thermodynamic consistency and cellular identification unresolved.

For the post hoc comparison with the gate and actin times exchanged, we held the organization response constant and used matrix exponentials to evaluate the two initial-value problems. This calculation illustrates that the transfer function is unchanged when the two filters exchange places. Both time constants were specified in the model.

### Two-compartment organization and shared actin and deformation dynamics

Two equal-area compartments had concentrations \(c_\pm=m(1\pm r)\), with fixed dimensionless mean \(m\) and contrast \(0\le r\le1\). We chose \(m=0.3\) and 1.0 as illustrative regimes. The imposed signal \(u(t)\) was one from 20 to 200 s and zero elsewhere. The effective signaling variable \(p\) and accessibility \(a\) followed

\[
\dot p=(u-p)/\tau_p,\qquad \dot a=(0.5+0.4p-a)/\tau_a.
\]

Both variables are phenomenological. Measuring phosphorylation occupancy or a structural ensemble would require separate readouts. Uniform regulation fixed \(r=0\). Finite recruitment used

\[
\dot r=-(1-r^2)(r-Rp)/\tau_r,
\]

and the demixing analogue used

\[
\dot r=-(1-r^2)r(r^2-R^2p)/(\tau_rR^2).
\]

At fixed \(p\), the trajectories descend the stated convex recruitment and nonconvex demixing potentials, with nonnegative mobility \(1-r^2\). Both nonuniform models conserve the total regulator across their two compartments. The second law models symmetry breaking without resolving a spatial phase boundary. The initial contrast was 0.03 for both nonuniform models and zero for uniform regulation. With \(h(c)=c^2/(1+c^2)\) and \(H=[h(c_-)+h(c_+)]/2\), all three descriptions used the same downstream equations

\[
\dot b=(aH-b)/\tau_b,\qquad \dot x=(b-x)/\tau_x.
\]

The state \(b\) is the effective actin-dependent load, and \(x\) is dimensionless deformation. Positive \(\tau_x\) gives an overdamped spring–drag balance in normalized units. The chemical cost of maintaining \(b\) remains unspecified. The initial values of \(b\) and \(x\) were their corresponding pre-input steady values.

### How the synthetic organization classifiers were trained and tested

For each independent parameter set, we drew \(\tau_p,\tau_a,\tau_r,\tau_b,\tau_x\) uniformly from 6–12, 5–12, 8–20, 10–25 and 12–35 s, respectively, and \(R\) uniformly from 0.45–0.75. Each draw generated all three candidate classes at matched parameter values. These are assumed ranges for the synthetic test. At each concentration, we used distinct seeds for development and testing, with 300 independent parameter sets in each. A further 300 at each concentration were reserved for the driven-recruitment model and another 300 for the control with null coupling.

We integrated to 300 s with midpoint RK2 at step 0.5 s. At 0, 60, 120, 180, 240 and 300 s, each dimensionless position sample received independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.005. We sampled optional molecular contrast at the same times, using independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.03. The position features were five differences from the first sample. The contrast features were the six contrast samples. Model parameters and observation noise were assigned. The simplified observation model omits projection and uncertain timing within an image stack.

We trained separate pooled-covariance nearest-centroid classifiers for each concentration and choice of readout. The development data set the feature scaling and within-class covariance, with \(0.01I\) added before inversion. We evaluated the held-out trajectories using the fixed model, features and classifier. The 5,000 bootstrap replicates resampled independent parameter sets with all classes and channels paired within each set. The resulting intervals quantify finite-simulation variability.

### Imposed molecular redistribution and independent spatial tests

We modeled redistribution on a periodic one-dimensional interval of dimensionless length 32, sampled at 64 grid points. The zero-mean field \(\phi\) sets the positive dimensionless regulator concentration \(c=m(1+0.6\phi)\), with fixed mean \(m=0.3\) or 1.0. For each parameter draw, the model classes shared an initial field composed of modes 2, 3 and 4. The mode phases were drawn independently, and the common initial spatial standard deviation was drawn from 0.025–0.035. These are idealized redistributions. One model time unit is one assigned second for comparison with imaging cadence. Kinetic rates were uncalibrated.

The demixing field obeyed

\[
\partial_t\phi=M\partial_{xx}(\phi^3-\phi-\kappa\partial_{xx}\phi),
\]

with energy \(E=\int[(\phi^2-1)^2/4+\kappa(\partial_x\phi)^2/2]dx\). The passive recruitment comparator relaxed by diffusion, \(\partial_t\phi=D\partial_{xx}\phi\). The first active recruitment law followed \(\partial_t\phi=D_A\partial_{xx}\phi+\lambda[\phi-\phi^3-\langle\phi-\phi^3\rangle]\). The last term is a phenomenological source whose integral is zero. The source leaves the underlying reaction network unspecified. We drew \(M,\kappa,D,D_A,\lambda\) independently and uniformly over [0.16, 0.24], [0.9, 1.1], [0.1, 0.2], [0.1, 0.2] and [0.055, 0.075], respectively. The two readout times \(\tau_b,\tau_x\) were uniform on [10, 20] and [15, 25] assigned seconds. Each field drove the same local actin, spatial-average load and deformation readouts, \(\partial_tb=(0.7h(c)-b)/\tau_b\), \(F=\langle b\rangle\) and \(\dot x=(F-x)/\tau_x\). The effective actin-associated load \(F\) is dimensionless. Its coupling to deformation is imposed, with no mechanical feedback into the field.

For the first challenge, 200 independent draws per concentration generated paired trajectories across the three models. We added independent Gaussian field noise with standard deviation 0.002 at each grid point. The contrast statistic \(G_{60}\) divided the observed spatial standard deviation at 60 s by its value at time zero. The fixed rule was \(G_{60}>1.3\). After observing the result, we designed the second challenge using new parameter draws and errors. From initial modes 2 and 3, we calculated \(g_n=\log(|\widehat\phi_n(15)|/|\widehat\phi_n(0)|)/15\) and \(S=g_3-g_2\). The spectral rule was \(S>0\). The joint rule also required \(F(60)-F(0)>0\) at \(m=0.3\) or \(F(60)-F(0)<0\) at \(m=1.0\), based on noisy load measurements. Independent Gaussian load noise had standard deviation 0.0002 at 0 and 60 s. The sign test assumes known \(m\).

After the spectral challenge, we drew 200 new independent parameter sets per concentration for the active comparator with matched linear spectrum,

\[
\partial_t\phi=-M\partial_{xx}\phi-M\kappa\partial_{xxxx}\phi-\gamma[\phi^3-\langle\phi^3\rangle],
\]

with \(\gamma\) uniform on 0.05–0.15 in assigned reciprocal seconds. The cubic source is phenomenological, and the two models have different nonlinear dynamics. The energy supply and full nonequilibrium phase behavior of this active law remain unspecified. We left the classifier and threshold fixed in this test. Each extension was specified after the preceding result and before generating its own independent synthetic cases.

The solver stepped the periodic finite-difference Laplacian eigenmodes at 0.25 s, treating the highest-order term implicitly. We checked discrete demixing energy at every step at fixed parameters and checked mass and concentration positivity for all cases. For one reference from each original class, reducing the step to 0.125 s and increasing the grid to 128 points changed \(\phi\) at sampled times by at most 0.00336 and 0.00923, respectively, against a tolerance of 0.02 fixed in advance. The respective largest load or deformation differences were 9.36×10⁻⁶ and 6.22×10⁻⁶ against the 0.002 tolerance. The separate active reference with matched linear spectrum passed the same checks, with maximum field differences 0.00313 and 0.00872. A small-amplitude Fourier mode reproduced the semidiscrete linear growth rates within 2.57×10⁻⁴ per assigned second. These checks cover the stated reference runs. Wilson intervals describe error rates conditional on the synthetic design and retain uncertainty even when zero false positives are observed.

### Independent test with three perturbation amplitudes

After the active comparator passed the spectral rule, we specified a further test with independent parameter draws and noise. For both operators and all three perturbations in each draw, we shared the same parameters and phase. Perturbations were sinusoidal at spatial mode 2, with \(A=0.12\), 0.28 and 0.40. The passive model used a cubic coefficient \(\beta\) drawn uniformly from 0.7–1.3. The active model used \(\gamma=M\beta\lambda_6\), matching the leading passive third-harmonic source. Here \(\lambda_n=4\sin^2(\pi n/N)/\Delta x^2\) is the positive eigenvalue of the negative discrete Laplacian, \(N=64\) and \(\Delta x=32/N\). Let \(Z_n(t)\) be the unnormalized discrete Fourier coefficient of the noisy field. For each amplitude, \(g_2(A)=\log(|Z_2(5)|/|Z_2(0)|)/5\) gives the growth rate of the imposed mode over the first 5 s. The negative least-squares slope of \(g_2(A)\) against \(A^2\) gave \(\widehat C_1\), the coefficient of amplitude-dependent attenuation. At \(A=0.40\), we estimated third-harmonic generation after alignment to the input phase \(\theta\) as

\[
\widehat C_3
=-\frac{2\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl([Z_6(5)-Z_6(0)]e^{-3i\theta}\bigr)}{5N A^3}.
\]

The fixed classification required \(\widehat C_1>0\) and \(Q=\widehat C_3/\widehat C_1>1.2\). ### Optical calibration and local stability calculations

The synthetic optical calibration in the main Methods and Supplementary Results estimates recovery of the source ratio for a linear detector with known background. This calibration concerns detector-channel mixing. Measuring sensitivity to the gate and actin states, and the delay and bleaching of the sensor, requires independent information.

For the common-input calculation, subtraction of the feed-forward trajectories gives a constant triangular difference system. Its eigenvalues give the asymptotic decay rates under the prescribed input, even with a nonlinear sigmoid of that input. Coincident roots can contribute polynomial transients. We verified one assigned example using a matrix exponential. In the feedback model, differentiating the sigmoid at equilibrium gives \(K\), and the Jacobian determinant gives the characteristic polynomial \((\lambda+1)^3+K\). We compared analytical real parts with direct eigenvalues in two fixed cases. The feedback calculation establishes local stability properties of the specified equilibria. Attractor structure, chemical work, the mechanical constitutive law of cells and the molecular protein ensemble are outside its scope.
